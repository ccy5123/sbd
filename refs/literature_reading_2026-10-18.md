# Reading notes: composition, amount, sound and sulfur odorants (2026-10-18)

Source PDFs: author-supplied (`refs/pdfs_incoming/`, not tracked: publisher copyright). Extracted numbers only; page and
table references are to the PDFs. Molar masses (M, g/mol) and correlations marked "computed" were computed from the
printed tables (renormalised percentages; M: CO2 44.009, O2 31.998, N2 28.014, CH4 16.043, H2 2.016; dry air 28.86).

## A. Does composition co-vary with the amount of gas?

**Answer.** Yes, through the fraction of fermentation gas; not through density. Without a dietary challenge the sign
can reverse.

Fermentation fraction rises with amount under bean, fibre or lactulose challenges:
- Hammer 1993 Table II: flatus H2 5.0 → 28.6 → 44.8 % at 100 → 430 → 899 mL/6 h (fasting, 12.5 g, 25 g lactulose).
  Fig 2B: share of H2 leaving by breath falls from 90 % to 20 % as flatus volume rises (r = -0.94).
- Christl 1992 (Gastroenterology): 65 % of H2 to breath below 200 mL/day, 25 % above 500 mL/day.
- Levitt 1976 Table 1 (one patient, 6 conditions): fermentation 26 → 79 % at 55 → 720 mL/4 h; computed
  r(vol, CO2 %) = +0.94, r(vol, N2 %) = -0.70; p. 261: rapid excretion = H2 + CO2, slow = N2.
- Steggerda 1968 Table 1 (5 diets): computed r(vol, CO2 %) = +0.999, r(vol, N2 %) = -0.95; 15 → 176 cc/h.
- Steggerda 1966 Table III (4 diet groups): r(vol, CO2 %) = +0.97, r(vol, N2 %) = -0.98; CH4 is a stable subject trait.
- Calloway 1969 Table I (6 men): pooled r(vol, fermentation %) = +0.86.
- O'Donnell 1984 Table 3: r = +0.94 over 4 cells; Marthinsen 1982 Table 4: +0.88; Hickey 1972 Table 2: +0.87.
- Askevold 1956 (39 rows, N2 80–94 %): r(rate, CO2 %) = +0.73, r(rate, N2 %) = -0.49.

Density does not follow the amount:
- M ranges: Marthinsen 25.7–27.1; O'Donnell 25.9–28.1; Calloway within subject <= 1.1 g/mol change; Askevold
  27.5–29.0. r(vol, M): Marthinsen +0.25, O'Donnell -0.31, Hickey -0.37, Askevold -0.26; Steggerda 1968 +0.90
  (5 means), Levitt 1976 +0.54.
- CO2 (heavy) and H2 (light) rise together; M is set by the CO2:H2 ratio of the substrate (Hickey: apple juice
  344 mL/6 h, M 22.3; grape juice 184 mL/6 h, M 31.7).

Sign reverses without a challenge:
- Kirk 1949 Table 2 (20 normals, ordinary diet): computed r(mL/min, fermentation %) = -0.41, r(O2 %) = +0.46;
  pooled Tables 4–6 (n = 73) r = -0.28. Brussels sprouts raised volume mainly through N2 (livelier peristalsis).
- Levitt 1971 Table 1 (bowel content, n = 11): r(vol, N2 %) = +0.59, r(vol, CO2 %) = -0.76; CO2 nearly constant in mL.
- Levitt 1998: patient with > 50 passages/day, flatus 82–91 % N2 (swallowed air), M 28.5–30.1.

Passage size and frequency: Kirk 1949 p. 786, single emissions 25–100 mL, up to 8–10 per hour after dinner; Sutalf
1979: passages under ~7 mL felt as "a squeaker"; normal frequency ~10–14/day, upper limit 20/day (Levitt 1998).

Only Suarez 1997 pairs per-passage volume with per-passage composition.

## B. What sets audibility?

Anorectal mechanics during flatus:
- Miller 1988 (Br J Surg 75:1003): feeling flatus, sphincter pressure -30 mmHg (20–50), rectal +7 (0–15); passing
  flatus, -35 mmHg (0–88) and +8 (-10 to 88); rectal >= anal pressure in 80 %.
- Rønholt 1999 Table 1: flatus with anal relaxation of 29 ± 8 mmHg lasting 19 ± 6 s (93 % of passages).
- Kumar 1990: in 35/49 flatus events anal pressure ROSE by 20 mmHg (11–52) with external sphincter activity.
- Corsetti 2017: colonic pressurisations 15 ± 3 mmHg over 24 ± 4 s precede anal relaxation (23 ± 4 s).
- Mitchell 2012 Table 2: anal opening pressure at rest 51.5 ± 20.7 cmH2O (~5 kPa), opening elastance
  1.32 cmH2O/mm².
- Luft 2012, Sørensen 2014 (FLIP): mid-canal ~5 mm (probe floor) up to ~30–36 mmHg, 8.5–13 mm at 40–55 mmHg.
- Haas 2017: yield pressure 14.5 ± 12.1 mmHg (~1.9 kPa), incontinent women.
- Serra 2001: 45 ± 7 mL per evacuation (cannula); 22 ± 8 mL when held back.

Soft-tissue sphincter phonation (pharyngo-oesophageal segment) as the closest analogue:
- Nagai 1993: sustained oesophageal phonation at 11–80 cmH2O (lowest 1.08 kPa), 26–173 mL/s, f0 58–150 Hz,
  63–86 dB, 0.7–3.3 s.
- Grolman 2007 Table 2: minimum phonation 18 ± 7.8 cmH2O (1.77 kPa), 133 mL/s, 68 dB at 30 cm.
- Ng 2011 Table IV: 25.1 cmH2O at 70.5 mL/s (oesophageal), 22.6 at 134 mL/s (tracheoesophageal).
- Zhang 2019: intensity rises 0.4 dB/mmHg with pressure and 3.6 dB/mm with sphincter diameter.
- f0: Robbins 1984 65–83 Hz; Pindzola 1989 84–108 Hz; Deschler 1999 82–116 Hz; Moon 1987 19–154 Hz.
- Brass lips: Mattéoli 2021 threshold ~0.6 kPa (pedal note) to ~5.5 kPa; Bromage 2010 85 dB at 0.7 kPa.
- Gas density: MacLean 1966, heliox shifts formants, not f0. No onset-pressure data with gas density.

Evidence-based model ranges (proposal): T 0.2–20 s (audible events 0.2–2 s); d 1–8 mm; P_th 0.6–2.5 kPa (nominal
1.2–1.8); f0 60–250 Hz (nominal 80–120); driving pressure typically 0–2 kPa, tail to ~6 kPa. Recomputed silent
share with these ranges: 0.80–0.84 (current 0.79); robust band ~0.75–0.90; the unconstrained lower bound of d
(1 mm) is the largest lever.

Mechanistic caveat: flatus passes either through a relaxed sphincter with pressures equalised (driving ~1 kPa, near
or below P_th: plausibly silent; Miller, Rønholt) or against a contracted sphincter (opening 1.9–5 kPa, above
P_th: should oscillate; Kumar). The studies disagree on which dominates.

No study measures flatus sound with amount or composition.

## C. Sulfur odorants vs amount and bulk composition

Sulfur-targeted factors move sulfur without moving bulk gas:
- Suarez 1998 (bismuth) Table 3: faecal H2S 5740 → 249 nmol/g, MeSH 3781 → 961; H2, CH4, CO2 and total gas volume
  unchanged (p. 925).
- Suarez 1999 (charcoal) Table 2: H2S and MeSH -30 to -40 % in homogenates, CO2, H2, CH4 unchanged; Table 4: 15.7 vs
  15.3 passages/day (no change in vivo).
- Levine 1998 Table 2: H2S 3× higher in ulcerative colitis, CO2/H2/CH4 not different; Fig 3: CH4 vs H2S r = 0.112.
- Ohge 2005 (pouchitis): H2S 9-fold and MeSH 65-fold spread across groups, H2 not different.
- Giffard 2001 (dogs): charcoal/yucca/zinc cut H2S 71–86 %, total gas 17–20 vs 18 mL/100 g; episode count unchanged;
  bad/unbearable episodes 16.1 → 2.2 %.

Diet and the hydrogen sink couple them, negatively:
- Yao 2018 Table 1: across fibres, total gas 0.8–40 mL/g and H2S suppression 14–89 % (more gas, less H2S); FOS on
  cysteine raised CO2 and H2 12–16-fold while suppressing H2S 90 %. Cysteine +1557 % H2S, sulfate +10 %.
- Christl 1992 (Gut) Table III: dietary sulphate raised sulphide production 0.35 → 0.99 µmol/g/h and cut CH4
  production 1.43 → 0.29 in 3/6 subjects; faecal weight and transit unchanged (Table II).
- Gibson 1993 (FEMS) Table 2: in sulphidogenic gut contents H2S (3.3 µmol/g) replaces CH4 (3.8) near mole for mole.
- Grimble 1989 p. 11: sulphate reducers divert H2 away from CH4 (gas volume) towards non-volatile sulphide.
- Gibson 1990 Table IV: H2S production falls 7.6-fold from pH 7.5 to 5.5 (mechanism for fibre); but So 2023 found no
  in-vivo pH change with fibre.

Methanogen-sulphide exclusivity is not supported: Strocchi 1991 (sulphide 0.15 vs 0.18 mM, n.s.), Strocchi 1994
(methanogens suppress sulphide 6-fold), Florin 1991 (methanogenic faeces 0.77 vs 0.54 µmol/g total sulphide),
Levine 1998 (r = 0.112), Pitcher 2000 (coexistence in 3 subjects) all contradict Gibson 1990.

Faecal sulphide is set more by binding capacity than by production (Levitt 2002; free:total 1:159–1:244), which
weakens faecal-sulphide diet studies (e.g. Magee 2000) as evidence for gas-phase H2S.

Kellingray 2017: a Brassica-rich diet lowered the relative abundance of sulphate-reducing bacteria (no gas measured).

Implication: within one diet, sulfur concentration is independent of passage volume (Suarez 1997). Across diets,
fermentable carbohydrate raises bulk gas while lowering H2S, so large fermentation-rich passages tend to carry less
sulfur. No in-vivo study measures both.
