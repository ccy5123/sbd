## Families

- **≥ B on traditional/lexical evidence:** 2 families (Indo-European, Sino-Tibetan); 4 if media-only A is counted (+ Austronesian, Koreanic).
- **Verified dedicated silent-fart word (≥ C):** 5 families (Indo-European, Sino-Tibetan, Japonic, Uralic, Afro-Asiatic).
- **Independent smell-defined silent words within Indo-European:** at least 4 (Germanic *fice/foist*, Slavic бздеть, Romance *vesse* < Latin *vissire*, Iranian چس).

Draft sentence for the manuscript: *"The silent/loud distinction is lexicalised in at least five language families; the stronger claim that the silent variant smells worse is asserted explicitly in Chinese and English folk sayings and encoded in the definitions of smell-specific 'silent' words that arose independently at least four times within Indo-European."*

## PIE *pesd- vs *perd-

Supported, not settled. LIV² glosses *pesd- "to fart softly" and *perd- "to fart loudly" (via Wiktionary; independently cited by the Dutch EWN), and Pokorny glosses pezd- "to break wind (softly)". Daughter languages are mixed (Latin *pēdō* < *pesd- became the ordinary word; Lithuanian *bezdėti*, Czech *bzdít* mean just "fart"). Manuscript wording: "a soft/loud split is reconstructed in the standard handbooks", not "attested for 5,000 years". To verify before submission: Mallory & Adams (1997) s.v. *fart*; LIV² pp. 473, 477 directly.

## Notable side findings

- *pesd-* reflexes drift towards "stink" (Serbo-Croatian *bazdjeti*), "fear" (Russian, Lithuanian), "nonsense" (Polish *bzdura*) and "puffball" (Greek πέζις; independently French *vesse-de-loup*, Italian *vescia*).
- Classical Arabic jurisprudence (Bukhari 135/137) treats sound OR smell as alternative detection signs — a two-channel detection model 1,200 years before ours.
- The HKUST *Science Focus* article explicitly disputes the Chinese proverb.
- Grey literature counter-evidence: Flatology "Sound of Stink" (ICEF, 2020) reports n = 1,619 with a weak *positive* loudness–smell relation (R² = 0.0155). Non-peer-reviewed, possibly satirical.
