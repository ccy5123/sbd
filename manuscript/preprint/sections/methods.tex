\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

All parameters, with their sources, ranges and status (verified, derived, partial or assumption), are kept in one file
from which Table~\ref{SI-tab:params} (Supporting Information) is generated. Code, data and figures are in the accompanying archive.

\subsection{Source}
\label{sec:source}
Passage volumes span 17--375\,mL, with a median of 92\,mL \cite{suarez1997}. In the Monte Carlo analysis, volume $V$,
duration $T$ (0.3--5\,s) and effective orifice diameter $d$ (1--15\,mm) are drawn log-uniformly; the last two are
assumptions, since neither has been measured.

Sulfur gas concentrations are taken from 87 passages of 16 volunteers \cite{suarez1998}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \ce{H2S} 1.06\,\textmu mol\,L$^{-1}$;
  \item methanethiol 0.21\,\textmu mol\,L$^{-1}$;
  \item dimethyl sulfide 0.08\,\textmu mol\,L$^{-1}$.
\end{itemize}
They vary between passages by a log-normal factor with $\sigma_{\ln} = 1.1$, derived from the reported standard
errors. The factor is drawn independently of volume, because sulfur concentration does not correlate with passage
volume \cite{suarez1997}.

Buoyancy is signed. Reduced gravity is computed from measured compositions; it is positive for the median passage but
negative for \ce{CO2}-rich ones.

\subsection{Odorant chemistry}
\label{sec:methods-chem}
\emph{Odour activity.} Each sulfur gas contributes an odour activity value $\mathrm{OAV}_i = x_i/\theta_i$, its mole
fraction over its detection threshold, and the passage's OAV is their sum (additivity is assumed; mixture suppression
is not modelled). We compare two measured compositions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item 87 passages of 16 volunteers \cite{suarez1998}, used throughout the dispersion model;
  \item 28 passages measured by gas chromatography: \ce{H2S} 149, methanethiol 216 and dimethyl sulfide 144\,nmol\,L$^{-1}$
  \cite{tangerman2009}.
\end{itemize}
Three threshold sets bracket the observer: a trained panel \cite{nagata2003}, population geometric means
\cite{amoore1983,wilby1969} and the lowest values in the literature, as compiled in ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009}.

\emph{Removal check.} Zinc acetate precipitates \ce{H2S} and thiols; activated charcoal adsorbs all three gases. For both
treatments, ref.~\citenum{suarez1998} reports the gas concentrations and two judges' ratings before and after. We
predict the rating ratio as $(\mathrm{OAV}_\mathrm{after}/\mathrm{OAV}_\mathrm{before})^n$ with $n = 0.20$--$0.45$
\cite{collins2001}. This is a consistency check of the intensity law, not a fit.

\emph{Buoyancy.} The reduced gravity $g' = g(\rho_a-\rho_g)/\rho_a$ of each of 16 measured compositions
\cite{suarez1997} is split into two parts:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a thermal part, $g(1-T_a/T_g)$, for gas at 37\,\textdegree C in air at 22\,\textdegree C;
  \item a compositional part, $g(M_a-M_g)/M_a$, at equal temperature, with air at 50\% relative humidity.
\end{itemize}
Whether flatus is saturated with water vapour has not been measured, so we bracket dry and saturated gas
[ASSUMPTION].

\emph{Conserved tracers.} Gas-phase losses use IUPAC rate constants at 298\,K for reaction with OH, \ce{O3}, \ce{NO3}
and Cl \cite{atkinson2004}. Oxidant levels come from direct measurements in an office: OH $0.6$--$9.8\times10^{5}$\,cm$^{-3}$
and \ce{O3} 0.7--2.6\,ppb \cite{fiorentino2023}. A worst case adds OH $10^6$\,cm$^{-3}$ with \ce{O3} 40\,ppb, and Cl
$10^6$\,cm$^{-3}$ during hypochlorite cleaning [ASSUMPTION]. No deposition velocity $v_d$ has been published for these
gases on indoor surfaces, so surface loss is bracketed as $k = v_d\,S/V$ with $S/V = 3$\,m$^{-1}$.

\emph{Dilution anchor.} Ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009} reports the lowest source concentration that every panel member
detected when 100\,mL was released below the nose. Divided by a detection threshold, it gives the dilution between
release and nose in that test. We compare it with the breath-averaged dilution of the analytic model.
\src{chem/chem\_report.md}

\subsection{Acoustics}
\label{sec:acoustics}
The anal canal is modelled as a soft-tissue lip reed. The valve self-oscillates when the driving pressure
$\tfrac12\rho(u_0/C_\mathrm{d})^2$ exceeds an oscillation threshold $P_\mathrm{th}$, which is anchored to vocal-fold and
brass-lip thresholds (0.2--1.3\,kPa); the discharge coefficient is $C_\mathrm{d} = 0.85$. This gives an onset velocity
$u_\mathrm{onset} \approx 30$\,m\,s$^{-1}$ (14--51).

An oscillating valve radiates as a harmonic monopole: the fundamental is at 160\,Hz and the harmonics roll off at
$-12$\,dB per octave. A release counts as \emph{audible} when at least one harmonic exceeds both the ISO 226 hearing
threshold \cite{iso226_2023} and the critical-ratio masked threshold of the background \cite{hawkins1950}. Backgrounds
range from a 35\,dB(A) living room to a 77\,dB(A) subway car \src{acoustics/m2\_report.md}.

\subsection{Analytic dispersion}
\label{sec:gate0}
Dispersion is modelled in two phases.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Near field.} The release behaves as a starting jet. Its tip follows
  $x = \Gamma (\dot Q_0 u_0)^{1/4} t^{1/2}$, and once the source stops it follows the puff law
  $x^4 = C_p^4 Q_0 u_0 t$, with $\Gamma = 2.8$ and $C_p = 2.6$ \cite{sangras2002,diez2003}. The centreline dilution
  is $K_c d^*/x$ with $K_c = 5.11$. In parallel, a buoyant-thermal branch grows with entrainment coefficient
  $\alpha = 0.25$ \cite{landeau2014}, and the larger of the two dilutions is kept. This phase ends at the hand-off,
  when the self-induced velocity has fallen to the ambient velocity $u_h$ (0.05--0.25\,m\,s$^{-1}$), which gives
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:silencer}.
  \item \emph{Transport.} The handed-over cloud is advected to the nose and spreads as a Gaussian puff,
  $\sigma^2 = \sigma_0^2 + 2Kt$. The indoor eddy diffusivity $K$ is $10^{-4}$--$1.3\times10^{-2}$\,m$^2$\,s$^{-1}$
  \cite{cheng2011}.
\end{enumerate}

Receptors and exposure:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the releaser's own nose, reached by the body's convective boundary layer \cite{licina2014}, with the
  transport speed taken from the CFD trajectories below;
  \item a neighbour 0.8--1.4\,m away, reached by room air.
\end{itemize}
Concentrations are averaged over one inspiration.

\emph{Monte Carlo.} We draw 20\,000 pairs of silent and audible releases. Each pair is classified by the acoustic
onset and evaluated with environmental parameters drawn from their ranges \src{gate0/gate0\_report.md}. Every
simplification is chosen to favour the silent release:
\begin{itemize}
  \item turbulent-jet constants are used even for laminar silent releases;
  \item the nose sits on the cloud centreline;
  \item the centreline concentration is used.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Computational fluid dynamics}
\label{sec:cfd}
\emph{Solver.} We use OpenFOAM 13 with transient RANS and the $k$--$\omega$ SST model. Buoyancy follows the Boussinesq
approximation, and the energy equation is solved for internal energy: the enthalpy form, which contains $p/\rho$,
drove a pressure--expansion instability through the pressure-relief vent.

\emph{Validation} (Section~\ref{SI-sec:si-validation}). A free round jet at $\mathrm{Re} = 6700$ reproduces the
canonical decay constants (velocity 5.0, scalar $K_c = 4.3$--4.6) with a grid uncertainty of at most 3.7\%. The
convective boundary layer of a seated manikin is 40--56\% faster than thermal-manikin data \cite{licina2014} but
follows their trend with room temperature. Both biases favour the silent release: excess jet spreading over-dilutes
audible releases by about 15\%, and the fast boundary layer shortens travel times to the releaser's own nose by about
30\%, for which the analytic model compensates.

\emph{Scenes} (Section~\ref{SI-sec:si-scenes}). Manikins are built from geometric primitives with skin at
33\,\textdegree C in rooms at 23\,\textdegree C:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a sofa duet: two seated people 0.6\,m apart in a 3.2 $\times$ 3.2 $\times$ 2.6\,m living room, in three
  postures of the releaser, over 30\,s (upright also 120\,s);
  \item an elevator: four standing people in a closed 630\,kg car, fan off, over 60\,s, with the culprit in the front
  or the back row;
  \item a standing pair: two people face to face, noses 1.0\,m apart, in the living room, in still air and with
  office ceiling ventilation at 2.9 air changes per hour (EN 16798-1 Category II \cite{olesen2024}), over 60\,s.
\end{itemize}
Each scene first develops its body plumes in a precursor run. Silent and audible branches then start from the same
field and release the same 92\,mL behind the releaser's buttocks as a momentum-equivalent inlet: silent, 2\,s through
8\,mm at $u_0 = 0.9$\,m\,s$^{-1}$; audible, 0.5\,s through 2.4\,mm at 41\,m\,s$^{-1}$. The odorant is a passive
scalar, sampled at probes 2.5\,cm in front of each nose, on 5\,cm breathing-zone discs and over sinusoidal breathing
cycles.

\subsection{Perception and observers}
\label{sec:perception}
\emph{Exposure and intensity.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The nose samples only during inhalation: sinusoidal flow at 12 breaths per minute, inspiratory fraction 0.42
  \cite{wysocki2006}.
  \item Perceived intensity follows Stevens' law with $n = 0.28$, measured for flatus \ce{H2S} \cite{collins2001}.
  \item Two passages are distinguishable when their concentrations differ by more than the Weber fraction 0.28
  \cite{stone1965}, i.e.\ a just-noticeable ratio of 1.28.
\end{itemize}
\src{perception/m6\_report.md}

\emph{Detection.} The odour thresholds in the dispersion model come from a trained panel \cite{nagata2003}. For an
untrained observer we use three ingredients:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Population thresholds}, 10--23 times higher \cite{amoore1983,wilby1969}.
  \item \emph{Spread.} Detection is a per-passage probability $\Phi(\log_2\mathrm{OAV}/\sigma)$ with $\sigma = 2$
  binary steps \cite{amoore1983}. One person's threshold varies over time about as much as thresholds vary across
  people \cite{stevens1988}.
  \item \emph{Distraction.} A bystander who is not expecting an odour needs 26 times the threshold for a 50\% response,
  with $\sigma = 3.5$ binary steps \cite{amoore1983,whisman1978}.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Intensity is unaffected by attention.} Distraction reduces noticing \cite{forster2018} but not the peak rated
intensity \cite{fallon2018}. Intensity is therefore always computed from the attentive OAV.

\emph{Observer variants.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Clothing removes 22\% of the sulfide gas \cite{ohge2005}.
  \item A sound can turn a distracted bystander into an attentive one for that passage.
\end{itemize}
\src{uq/observer\_ps.py}

\subsection{Uncertainty and the regime map}
\label{sec:uq}
The analytic model serves as the surrogate for uncertainty quantification. A Latin-hypercube CFD design was
unaffordable: one branch costs 1--2\,h on four cores.
\begin{itemize}
  \item First-order Sobol indices of $\log_{10} R$ are estimated with a given-data (binning) estimator, because the
  rejection sampling of silent and audible pairs makes the inputs dependent. The indices are therefore descriptive.
  \item Bootstrap gives 90\% intervals.
  \item The regime map shows $\log_{10} R$ against the audible release's Silencer number and the travel time, at equal
  mass.
\end{itemize}
\src{uq/m7\_report.md}

\emph{Routing-aware headline.} The CFD gives each scene's route for one release volume at equal mass. The odorant is a
passive scalar and the OAV is linear in concentration, so a passage of volume $V$ following the same route gives
$\mathrm{OAV}\times V/92$\,mL. For each of the 20\,000 analytic-model pairs we scale the silent and the audible CFD
doses by the pair's two volumes, and report $P(R>1)$ and the perceptual split per scene and nose. In the analytic
model this factorisation is exact ($R_\text{as emitted} = R_\text{equal mass}\,V_s/V_l$). Applied to the CFD it
assumes that the route does not depend on the volume, which holds for silent releases (whose momentum is negligible
against the body plume) and is an approximation for audible ones [APPROXIMATION] \src{uq/routing\_headline.py}.
