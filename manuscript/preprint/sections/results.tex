\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Most releases are physically silent}
\label{sec:res-acoustics}
Over the release prior, 79\% of passages never drive the valve into oscillation (regime S1;
Figure~\ref{fig:acoustics}). The remaining 21\% are audible in any quiet room.

The intermediate regime, an oscillating but inaudible release, is empty. At sofa distance (0.6\,m), even
1\,mL\,s$^{-1}$ of oscillating flow gives a 32\,dB fundamental, against an 18\,dB hearing threshold at 160\,Hz. A
``silent'' passage is therefore either physically silent or masked.

Masking needs loud backgrounds. In a restaurant or a subway car, only near-maximal passages (0.6--1.7\,L\,s$^{-1}$)
are still heard. Audible releases are fast: the median exit velocity is 68\,m\,s$^{-1}$ through a 1.6\,mm orifice
\src{acoustics/m2\_report.md}.

\subsection{Validation}
\label{sec:res-validation}
The jet constants and the convective boundary layer behave as stated in Section~\ref{sec:cfd}. Their biases matter
here in two ways.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Excess jet spreading over-dilutes audible releases by about 15\%, which favours the silent release.
  \item The fast convective boundary layer shortens travel times to the releaser's own nose by about 30\%. The analytic
  model compensates by stretching the CFD times by a factor of 1.4.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The physical ratio}
\label{sec:res-ratio}
Table~\ref{tab:ratio} gives $R = C_\mathrm{silent}/C_\mathrm{audible}$, breath-averaged, for 20\,000 random pairs.

\midrule
own nose & equal mass & 1.73 & 0.94--14.0 & 0.93 \\
own nose & as emitted & 1.15 & 0.20--6.0 & 0.56 \\
neighbour & equal mass & 1.35 & 0.97--8.7 & 0.93 \\
neighbour & as emitted & 0.92 & 0.15--5.0 & 0.47 \\
neighbour, denim & as emitted & 0.63 & 0.11--3.4 & 0.31 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\emph{Equal mass.} At equal mass a silent release is delivered more efficiently, as Eq.~\eqref{eq:silencer}
predicts: the audible release has been pre-diluted by its own momentum. Applying the just-noticeable ratio of 1.28,
54\% of these pairs are perceptibly stronger for the silent release at the neighbour and 44\% are indistinguishable
\src{uq/m7\_report.md}.

\emph{As emitted.} Passages as emitted reverse the picture, because loud passages are larger. At the neighbour a silent
passage is stronger in 47\% of pairs, i.e.\ a coin flip. Only 37\% are perceptibly stronger, against 44\% perceptibly
weaker. Trousers strip the jet momentum from both releases and push $P(R>1)$ down to 0.31.

\emph{What decides it.} As emitted, the two passage volumes explain about 0.6 of the variance of $\log_{10} R$ at the
neighbour. At equal mass, the eddy diffusivity $K$ explains 0.35. Sphincter pressure, discharge coefficient, buoyancy
and inhalation time each explain less than 0.5\%: the valve decides whether a passage is heard, not how strongly it
smells.

\emph{Regime map} (Figure~\ref{fig:uq}a). The silent advantage is perceptible only at short travel times and high
audible Silencer numbers. At the releaser's own nose (6.7\,s) every audible release is perceptibly weaker at equal
mass. At the neighbour (12.5\,s) a barely audible release is indistinguishable from a silent one.

\subsection{A sofa}
\label{sec:res-sofa}
The simulations of two people on a sofa overturn the analytic picture, in the direction opposite to the belief
(Table~\ref{tab:sofa}, Figure~\ref{fig:sofa}).

\emph{Silent release.} The silent release has too little momentum to leave the convective boundary layer of the
releaser's back. It is drawn up the gap between back and backrest, over the head and on to the ceiling. Within 30\,s
its breathing-zone OAV stays at or below $10^{-10}$ at both noses, while the region within 25\,cm of the releaser's head
reaches an OAV of 6\,000.

\emph{Audible release.} The audible jet escapes this chimney. It reaches the neighbour's head region within 6\,s and
both breathing zones at OAV 0.2--0.6.

\emph{Reclined posture.} Reclining the releaser by 4.5\,\textdegree\ closes the back onto the backrest. The silent
release then leaves the seat-level wedge sideways and still rises behind the head, so no breathing zone receives a
detectable dose. The audible jet now wraps around the releaser's face, exceeding threshold at their own nose (OAV
2--4).

In both postures the breathing-zone ratio is $R \approx 10^{-11}$--$10^{-14}$
\src{cfd/cases/sofa/m4\_report.md}. \gap{120 s window result}

\subsection{Perception}
\label{sec:res-perception}
\emph{Breath sampling.} Breath sampling barely protects an unwarned neighbour: the worst breath still captures 81--99\%
of the peak. A warned listener needs about 23\,s of breath-hold for an even chance of missing the cloud
\src{perception/m6\_report.md}.

\emph{Silent versus audible.} Through Stevens' law, the median physical difference at the neighbour becomes a 2.7\%
difference in perceived intensity. That is below the olfactory just-noticeable difference of about 7\% in intensity.
Without an expectation effect, a silent passage seems stronger in 47\% of pairs.

\emph{Expectation.} An expectation effect large enough to flip this would have to exceed 3\% in intensity. For brief
exposures the measured label effects are 0--3\%, and their sign for an unwarned listener is not established
\cite{herz2001,djordjevic2008,dalton1999}. We therefore keep expectation as an auxiliary hypothesis only.

\subsection{The composition counter-argument}
\label{sec:res-composition}
Could loud passages be mostly swallowed air and therefore odourless? Across 87 passages, \ce{H2S} concentration does
not correlate with passage volume ($r = +0.20$, not significant), so sulfur \emph{mass} rises with volume
\cite{suarez1997}.

The nitrogen in flatus is largely blood-derived rather than swallowed \cite{levitt1970}. At high production rates
almost all flatus is fermentation gas \cite{tomlin1991}. The counter-argument has no support.

\subsection{Memory}
\label{sec:res-memory}
Applying Theorem~3 to the simulated passages (Figure~\ref{fig:memory}) gives the following.

\emph{Gate-0 observer.} For an observer with trained-panel thresholds and the cloud centred on the nose, 93\% of passages
are smelled. The remembered belief is then 0.53, against a truth of 0.49.

\emph{Untrained observers.} Literature observer models lower the share of passages smelled, and the belief rises:

\begin{table}
\caption{Base rate $p_S$ and remembered belief $P(\text{a silent passage seems stronger})$ for literature observer
models; the cloud is centred on the neighbour's nose, so each $p_S$ is an upper bound.}
\label{tab:observers}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
observer & $p_S$ & belief & truth \\
\midrule
trained panel, deterministic & 0.93 & 0.53 & 0.49 \\
attentive, population thresholds & 0.59--0.68 & 0.67--0.73 & 0.50 \\
distracted (everyday) & 0.21 & 0.91 & 0.50 \\
distracted, clothed emitter & 0.19 & 0.92 & 0.51 \\
distracted, sound cues attention & 0.31 & 0.81 & 0.32 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\emph{Sound-cued attention.} When a sound makes the bystander attentive, audible passages are smelled more often, the
selection gap shrinks and the belief falls to 0.81. The ``truth'' in that row is lower, because that observer really
does smell audible passages more often.

\emph{The emitter.} The emitter, who notices every release, shows no selection bias (Figure~\ref{fig:memory}a).

\emph{Masking.} Masking is not a clean test of the mechanism: in loud backgrounds only the largest passages are heard,
and this ``loudness selection'' weakens the belief on its own.
