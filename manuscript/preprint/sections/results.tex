\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The odorants and the gas that carries them}
\label{sec:res-composition}
Over two measured compositions and three threshold sets, methanethiol carries 43--94\% of the summed OAV and never
less than \ce{H2S} (Section~\ref{SI-sec:si-chem}). This differs from the conclusion drawn in
ref.~\citenum{suarez1998}, where \ce{H2S} is the main sulfur gas and the one whose concentration the judges' malodour
ratings followed. Weighted by odour thresholds, the same measured concentrations give methanethiol at least an equal
share: it is one fifth as concentrated, but its threshold is 5--6 times lower. Composition does not enter the
silent/audible comparison, because transport is linear and acts on all three gases alike. Stevens' law with these
OAVs predicts the judges' ratings of zinc-acetate-treated flatus (0.31--0.59 predicted, 0.28 and 0.59 rated) without
fitting.

Composition, not temperature, sets most of the buoyancy: after cooling to room temperature 25--38\% of passages are
heavier than air. Gas-phase oxidation removes at most 0.4\% of any odorant within 120\,s
(Table~\ref{SI-tab:reactivity}).

\subsection{Most releases are physically silent}
\label{sec:res-acoustics}
In the lip-reed model, 85\% of the releases sampled never set the valve oscillating (Figure~\ref{fig:acoustics}); the
other 15\% are audible in any quiet room. No release in the sampled range oscillates without being heard in a quiet
room: the smallest oscillating release, about 33\,mL\,s$^{-1}$ through a 1\,mm orifice, gives a 47\,dB fundamental at
sofa distance (0.6\,m), against an 18\,dB hearing threshold at 160\,Hz. A release that is not heard is therefore either physically silent or masked by
background noise.

Masking needs loud backgrounds: in a restaurant or a subway car only near-maximal passages are heard (0--13\% of
releases, depending on pitch). Audible releases are fast, with a median exit velocity of 88\,m\,s$^{-1}$.

\subsection{The ratio in the analytic model}
\label{sec:res-ratio}
The analytic model sends every cloud to the nose, so it bounds what dilution alone can do. Table~\ref{tab:ratio}
gives the breath-averaged ratio $R = C_\mathrm{silent}/C_\mathrm{audible}$ for 20\,000 random
pairs.

\emph{Equal mass.} At equal mass the audible release arrives pre-diluted by its own momentum
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:silencer}), and the silent one is stronger at the neighbour in 93\% of pairs. This is the
near-field exception of Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear}, not a violation of Proposition~1, which holds only once the
release has lost its own momentum. At the just-noticeable
ratio of 1.28, the silent release is perceptibly stronger in 56\% of pairs and indistinguishable in 42\%.

\emph{As emitted.} Audible passages are larger, and at the neighbour a silent passage is stronger in 45\% of pairs:
perceptibly stronger in 35\%, perceptibly weaker in 45\%. Trousers remove the jet momentum of both releases and lower
$P(R>1)$ to 0.28.

\emph{Which inputs matter} (Figure~\ref{fig:uq}b). The two passage volumes explain about two-thirds of the variance of
$\log_{10} R$ as emitted; the valve parameters set whether a passage is heard, but hardly how strongly it smells.

\emph{Regime map} (Figure~\ref{fig:uq}a). The silent release is perceptibly stronger only at short travel times and
high Silencer numbers of the audible release. At equal mass even a barely audible release is perceptibly
weaker than a silent one, at the releaser's own nose (6.7\,s; $R = 1.61$ at the acoustic onset) and at the neighbour
(12.5\,s; $R = 1.33$).

\subsection{Real rooms: where the release goes}
\label{sec:res-rooms}
In the simulated rooms the warm air rising along the
body takes a slow release with it, while a fast jet leaves it, and the two then reach different noses.

\emph{Sofa.} The silent release rises along the releaser's back, over the head and to the ceiling, while the audible
jet spreads along the seat to the neighbour (Figure~\ref{fig:planes}a,b). At the neighbour, the worst breath over 150\,s (volumes as emitted) is 0.07
and 0.09 for the two silent releases and 0.16 and 16.9 for the audible ones; reclining or leaning forward moves the
silent values to 0.12--0.95, still below the trained-panel threshold. The dose rises with the ratio of the release's
momentum flux to that of the boundary layer it enters, $\Pi = M_0/m_\mathrm{p}$, where $m_\mathrm{p}$ is the
upward momentum flux of the boundary layer 15\,cm above the release point: a release must break out of the
boundary layer to reach a neighbour (Figure~\ref{fig:routing}a).

\emph{Standing pair.} Face to face and bare, the neighbour stays below OAV 0.2 for every release, in still air and
with ventilation, whatever $\Pi$: the body plumes carry both clouds upwards between the two people. Momentum is therefore
not the only variable; the arrangement of the bodies is the second.

\emph{Lift.} In a closed car with a person behind the releaser, the silent release rises in the 0.2\,m gap into
that person's breathing zone (Figure~\ref{fig:planes}c): worst breath 260 for the median silent release, against 36 for the strongest audible
one. Every nose in the car passes OAV 1.

\emph{Releaser.} At the releaser's own nose the relation is not monotonic (Figure~\ref{fig:routing}b): the highest
doses at equal mass, up to OAV 70, come from releases with $\Pi \approx 10$--100, which leave the seat region but stay close to the
body.

\subsection{Which passage is stronger when smelled}
\label{sec:res-headline}
Table~\ref{tab:headline} gives, for passages as emitted, how often each class is smelled and, among pairs in which
both are smelled, which is stronger. On the sofa a bystander rarely smells either, and the audible passage seven
times as often as the silent one (4.1 against 0.57\%). When both are smelled, which happens in 1 of about 3\,000
pairs, the audible one is stronger twice as often (59 against 30\%). In the closed lift the person directly behind
the releaser smells both in 39\% of pairs, and the silent one is the stronger more often (55 against 33\%): the
slow release rises along the releaser's back into that person's breathing zone. For the people beside and diagonally
behind the releaser it is the reverse (31--33 against 55--56\%). Between two standing people almost nothing reaches the
other nose. Weighted by time, the day average is 48 against 41\%; because both passages are so much more often
smelled in the lift, the lift supplies three-quarters of the pairs behind this average, and home-centred days, which
follow the sofa, give 32 against 57\%. At the releaser's own nose the two are about equal (44 against 45\%).

\emph{Robustness} (Section~\ref{SI-sec:si-robust}, Table~\ref{SI-tab:combined}). The lift result, carried by the person behind the
releaser, holds under every variation tested. The sofa result does not: the mesh-refinement factor of the releaser's nose (3.0 on every silent
dose) brings it to 42 against 46\%, and combined with the median large-eddy peak factors (2.2 silent, 1.3 audible)
it reverses (49 against 40\%), as it does with the measured release-order effect and the median peak factors (46
against 42\%). The just-noticeable ratio (1.15--1.45) moves only the ties. The day average follows whichever of sofa
and lift supplies more of the doubly smelled pairs, so it reverses when detection is made steeper or deterministic
and without the driving-pressure limit (41 against 48\%). In trousers, on the scenes run clothed (upright sofa and
standing pair), the audible passage is stronger in 91\% against 80\% bare.
\subsection{Perception}
\label{sec:res-perception}
\emph{Breath sampling.} For a neighbour who is not warned, the worst breath still captures 80--99\% of the peak. A
listener warned by the sound needs about 25\,s of breath-hold for an even chance of missing the cloud
(Figure~\ref{SI-fig:breath}).

\emph{Expectation} is not modelled. Labels change rated intensity by a few per cent for most odours
\cite{herz2001,djordjevic2008} and a health warning by 18--61\% \cite{dalton1999}, but every such study announced the
label, so the sign of the effect for an unwarned listener is unknown.

\subsection{Memory}
\label{sec:res-memory}
A bystander remembers a silent passage only if it was smelled. Among remembered pairs, the silent passage seems
worse in 91\% (attentive) and 98\% (distracted) of comparisons (Table~\ref{tab:headline}), while among pairs in
which both are smelled it is stronger in 48 and 51\%, and on the sofa in 30\%. The remembered share is not
fixed by definition: it falls as more passages are smelled and meets the truth, 31\%, when every passage is smelled
(Figure~\ref{fig:memory}). The releaser, who notices every release, shows no selection: memory equals the physical
comparison, silent stronger in about 44\% of the pairs in which both are smelled.
