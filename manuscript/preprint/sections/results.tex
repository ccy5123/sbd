\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The odorants and the gas that carries them}
\label{sec:res-composition}
\emph{Which molecule.} Methanethiol carries at least half of the odour and probably most of it
(Figure~\ref{fig:chem}a).
\begin{itemize}
  \item With the composition of ref.~\citenum{suarez1998}, methanethiol and \ce{H2S} share the OAV about equally.
  Methanethiol is one fifth as concentrated, but its threshold is 5--6 times lower.
  \item With the composition of ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009}, or with the lowest reported thresholds, methanethiol
  carries 60--94\%.
\end{itemize}
The two datasets disagree on the concentrations themselves: \ce{H2S} is five times methanethiol in one and slightly
below it in the other. That study attributes the difference to the bean-rich diet of the first
\cite{tangerman2009}. Its panel found that gas mixtures with methanethiol above its flatus threshold smelled like most
passages.

None of this affects the silent/audible comparison. Transport is linear and acts on all three gases alike, so every
ratio is the same for any composition. Absolute OAVs are 11--38\% lower with the second composition, well within the
uncertainty analysis.

\emph{The intensity law with real flatus.} Zinc acetate lowers the summed OAV by a factor of 14. Stevens' law then
predicts rating ratios of 0.31--0.59, and the two judges gave 0.28 and 0.59 \cite{suarez1998}. Activated charcoal
lowers the OAV about 260-fold and is rated far weaker than the power law predicts. This is the known failure of a power
law near threshold; it does not touch the supra-threshold ratios on which the silent/audible comparison rests.

\emph{What makes a passage rise or sink.} Composition, not temperature, sets the buoyancy (Figure~\ref{fig:chem}b).
\begin{itemize}
  \item For the median passage the compositional part ($+1.25$\,m\,s$^{-2}$ dry, $+1.40$ saturated) is about three
  times the thermal part ($+0.47$).
  \item \ce{H2} and \ce{CH4} make flatus light; \ce{CO2} makes it heavy. The compositional part falls with \ce{CO2}
  content ($r = -0.80$).
  \item 6--12\% of passages sink even while hot. After cooling to room temperature, 25--38\% are heavier than air.
\end{itemize}
The signed $g'$ used in the dispersion model follows from these compositions (Section~\ref{sec:source}).

\emph{Conserved tracers.} The odorants survive a passage (Table~\ref{tab:reactivity}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item In an office, gas-phase oxidation removes less than 0.4\% of any of the three gases in 120\,s. Even
  hypochlorite cleaning removes less than 4\%.
  \item Surface deposition is the one unconstrained sink. It removes 0.7\% in 120\,s at $v_d = 0.002$\,cm\,s$^{-1}$ but
  51\% at 0.2\,cm\,s$^{-1}$.
  \item Deposition would act most on the silent release, which lingers along the body and in the seat. It would
  therefore strengthen the routing results of Section~\ref{sec:res-rooms}, not weaken them.
\end{itemize}
Treating the odorants as passive scalars is justified against chemistry by two to three orders of magnitude, and
against deposition only if $v_d \lesssim 0.01$\,cm\,s$^{-1}$.

\emph{How dilute a passage arrives.} The panel test of ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009} implies a dilution of
$0.6$--$13\times10^{4}$ between release and nose, depending on the threshold set. The analytic model's
breath-averaged dilution at the neighbour has a median of $1.8\times10^{3}$ (10--90\%: $2.5\times10^{2}$--$2.9\times10^{4}$).
The anchor is biased upwards, because it requires the least sensitive panellist to detect but divides by the
thresholds of the most sensitive. The analytic model therefore sits at the concentrated end of what a real nose
receives, as an upper bound should, and agrees with the panel test to about an order of magnitude.

\emph{Are loud passages just swallowed air?} Across 87 passages, \ce{H2S} concentration does not correlate with
passage volume ($r = +0.20$, not significant), so sulfur \emph{mass} rises with volume \cite{suarez1997}. The nitrogen
in flatus is largely blood-derived rather than swallowed \cite{levitt1970}, and at high production rates almost all
flatus is fermentation gas \cite{tomlin1991}. The counter-argument has no support.

\subsection{Most releases are physically silent}
\label{sec:res-acoustics}
Over the release prior, 79\% of passages never drive the valve into oscillation (regime S1;
Figure~\ref{fig:acoustics}). The remaining 21\% are audible in any quiet room.

The intermediate regime, an oscillating but inaudible release, is empty. At sofa distance (0.6\,m), even
1\,mL\,s$^{-1}$ of oscillating flow gives a 32\,dB fundamental, against an 18\,dB hearing threshold at 160\,Hz. A
``silent'' passage is therefore either physically silent or masked.

Masking needs loud backgrounds. In a restaurant or a subway car an oscillating release must exceed
0.4--0.6\,L\,s$^{-1}$ at sofa distance (1.8--3.0\,L\,s$^{-1}$ at 3\,m) to be heard, so only near-maximal passages are. Audible releases are fast: the median exit velocity is 68\,m\,s$^{-1}$ through a 1.6\,mm orifice
\src{acoustics/m2\_report.md}.

\subsection{The physical ratio}
\label{sec:res-ratio}
Table~\ref{tab:ratio} gives $R = C_\mathrm{silent}/C_\mathrm{audible}$, breath-averaged, for 20\,000 random pairs.

\midrule

\bottomrule

\emph{Equal mass.} At equal mass a silent release is delivered more efficiently, as Eq.~\eqref{eq:silencer}
predicts: the audible release has been pre-diluted by its own momentum. Applying the just-noticeable ratio of 1.28,
54\% of these pairs are perceptibly stronger for the silent release at the neighbour and 44\% are indistinguishable
\src{uq/m7\_report.md}.

\emph{As emitted.} Passages as emitted reverse the picture, because loud passages are larger. At the neighbour a silent
passage is stronger in 47\% of pairs, i.e.\ a coin flip. Only 37\% are perceptibly stronger, against 44\% perceptibly
weaker. Trousers strip the jet momentum from both releases and push $P(R>1)$ down to 0.31.

\emph{What decides it.} As emitted, the two passage volumes explain about 0.6 of the variance of $\log_{10} R$ at the
neighbour. At equal mass, the eddy diffusivity $K$ explains 0.35. Sphincter pressure, discharge coefficient, buoyancy
and inhalation time each explain less than 0.5\%: the valve decides whether a passage is heard, not how strongly it
smells.

\emph{Regime map} (Figure~\ref{fig:uq}a). The silent advantage is perceptible only at short travel times and high
audible Silencer numbers. At the releaser's own nose (6.7\,s) every audible release is perceptibly weaker at equal
mass. At the neighbour (12.5\,s) a barely audible release is indistinguishable from a silent one.

\subsection{Real rooms: the route decides}
\label{sec:res-rooms}
The analytic model carries every cloud straight to the nose. In real rooms the body's own plume decides where a
release goes, and it treats the two modes differently: a silent release has too little momentum to leave it, an
audible jet escapes it. We simulated the same 92\,mL, released silently and audibly, in three scenes
(Section~\ref{SI-sec:si-scenes}).

\emph{Sofa.} The silent release is drawn up the gap between the releaser's back and the backrest, over the head and
on to the ceiling (Figure~\ref{fig:planes}). Within 30\,s its breathing-zone OAV stays at or below $10^{-10}$ at both
noses. The audible jet splashes into the corner between seat and backrest, spreads sideways along the gap and reaches
both breathing zones at OAV 0.2--0.6. Reclining by 4.5\,\textdegree\ or leaning forward by 10\,\textdegree\ does not
change this (Section~\ref{SI-sec:si-sofa}). After a minute of room recirculation both clouds return from the ceiling;
at the neighbour the two traces are then indistinguishable, as Theorem~2 requires, while the releaser's own nose sees
the mixed silent cloud 1.5--2 times higher, near threshold.

\emph{Elevator.} In a closed car with four standing occupants, what stands behind the culprit decides. With a person
behind (front-row culprit), the silent release rises in the narrow gap straight into that person's breathing zone,
and it is stronger at every nose ($R = 1.4$--$4.3$ at equal mass). With the car wall behind, it rises in the chimney
between back and wall, and the audible release is stronger at every nose ($R = 0.20$--$0.55$). With a front-row
culprit the person behind smells it first (8.8\,s, against 17.6\,s for the culprit), so the folk rule that the first
to smell it dealt it holds in only half of the positions. Judged by who reacts, onlookers identify the culprit barely
above chance (Section~\ref{SI-sec:si-elevator}).

\emph{Standing pair.} \gap{standing pair face to face, still air and office ventilation: results pending}

\emph{How often, as emitted.} Each scene fixes a route. Scaling its equal-mass doses by the volumes of passages as
emitted (Section~\ref{sec:uq}) gives the share of pairs in which the silent passage is stronger at each nose
(Figure~\ref{fig:routing}). Silent passages are also smaller (median volume ratio 0.47), so even where the route
favours the silent release the advantage shrinks: for the person behind a front-row culprit, a silent passage is
stronger in 37\% of pairs. Over the nine bystander noses $P(R>1)$ ranges from 0 to 0.72, with an equal-weight mean of
0.21, against 0.47 from the analytic model without routing \src{uq/routing\_headline.json}.

\subsection{Perception}
\label{sec:res-perception}
\emph{Breath sampling.} Breath sampling barely protects an unwarned neighbour: the worst breath still captures 81--99\%
of the peak. A warned listener needs about 23\,s of breath-hold for an even chance of missing the cloud
(Figure~\ref{SI-fig:breath}) \src{perception/m6\_report.md}.

\emph{Silent versus audible.} Through Stevens' law, the median physical difference at the neighbour becomes a 2.7\%
difference in perceived intensity. That is below the olfactory just-noticeable difference of about 7\% in intensity.
Without an expectation effect, a silent passage seems stronger in 47\% of pairs.

\emph{Expectation.} An expectation effect large enough to flip this would have to exceed 3\% in intensity. For brief
exposures the measured label effects are 0--3\%, and their sign for an unwarned listener is not established
\cite{herz2001,djordjevic2008,dalton1999}. We therefore keep expectation as an auxiliary hypothesis only.

\subsection{Memory}
\label{sec:res-memory}
Applying Theorem~3 to the simulated passages (Figure~\ref{fig:memory}) gives the following.

\emph{Gate-0 observer.} For an observer with trained-panel thresholds and the cloud centred on the nose, 93\% of passages
are smelled. The remembered belief is then 0.53, against a truth of 0.49.

\emph{Untrained observers.} Literature observer models lower the share of passages smelled, and the belief rises:

\begin{table}
\caption{Base rate $p_S$ and remembered belief $P(\text{a silent passage seems stronger})$ for literature observer
models; the cloud is centred on the neighbour's nose, so each $p_S$ is an upper bound.}
\label{tab:observers}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
observer & $p_S$ & belief & truth \\
\midrule
trained panel, deterministic & 0.93 & 0.53 & 0.49 \\
attentive, population thresholds & 0.59--0.68 & 0.67--0.73 & 0.50 \\
distracted (everyday) & 0.21 & 0.91 & 0.50 \\
distracted, clothed emitter & 0.19 & 0.92 & 0.51 \\
distracted, sound cues attention & 0.31 & 0.81 & 0.32 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\emph{Sound-cued attention.} When a sound makes the bystander attentive, audible passages are smelled more often, the
selection gap shrinks and the belief falls to 0.81. The ``truth'' in that row is lower, because that observer really
does smell audible passages more often.

\emph{The emitter.} The emitter, who notices every release, shows no selection bias (Figure~\ref{fig:memory}a).

\emph{Masking.} Masking is not a clean test of the mechanism: in loud backgrounds only the largest passages are heard,
and this ``loudness selection'' weakens the belief on its own.
