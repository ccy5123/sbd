\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The odorants and the gas that carries them}
\label{sec:res-composition}
\emph{Which molecule.} Over the two measured compositions and three threshold sets, methanethiol carries 43--94\% of
the summed OAV and never less than \ce{H2S} (Figure~\ref{fig:chem}a).
\begin{itemize}
  \item With the composition of ref.~\citenum{suarez1998}, methanethiol and \ce{H2S} carry about equal shares (43--54\%
  and 43--46\% with trained-panel and population thresholds). Methanethiol is one fifth as concentrated, but its
  threshold is 5--6 times lower.
  \item With the composition of ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009}, or with the lowest reported thresholds, methanethiol
  carries 60--94\%.
\end{itemize}
The two datasets disagree on the concentrations themselves: \ce{H2S} is five times methanethiol in one and slightly
below it in the other, a difference ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009} attributes to the bean-rich diet of the first. In
that study's panel test, gas mixtures with methanethiol above its flatus threshold smelled like most passages.

Composition does not enter the silent/audible comparison: transport is linear and acts on all three gases alike, so
every ratio is the same for any composition. Absolute OAVs are 11--38\% lower with the second composition.

\emph{The intensity law with real flatus.} Zinc acetate lowers the summed OAV by a factor of 14. Stevens' law then
predicts rating ratios of 0.31--0.59, and the two judges gave 0.28 and 0.59 \cite{suarez1998}. Activated charcoal
lowers the OAV about 260-fold and is rated far weaker than the power law predicts, as power laws fail near threshold.
The silent/audible comparison uses supra-threshold ratios, where the law held.

\emph{What makes a passage rise or sink.} Composition sets most of the buoyancy (Figure~\ref{fig:chem}b).
\begin{itemize}
  \item For the median passage the compositional part of the reduced gravity ($+1.25$\,m\,s$^{-2}$ dry, $+1.40$
  saturated) is about three times the thermal part ($+0.47$).
  \item \ce{H2} and \ce{CH4} make flatus light and \ce{CO2} makes it heavy; the compositional part falls with \ce{CO2}
  content ($r = -0.80$).
  \item 6--12\% of passages sink even while hot. After cooling to room temperature, 25--38\% are heavier than air.
\end{itemize}
The dispersion model uses these signed values of $g'$.

\emph{Losses on the way to the nose} (Table~\ref{tab:reactivity}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item In an office, gas-phase oxidation removes at most 0.4\% of any of the three gases in 120\,s, and hypochlorite
  cleaning less than 4\%.
  \item Surface deposition is unconstrained by data. It removes 0.7\% in 120\,s at $v_d = 0.002$\,cm\,s$^{-1}$ and 51\%
  at 0.2\,cm\,s$^{-1}$. Treating the odorants as passive scalars therefore holds against chemistry by two to three
  orders of magnitude, and against deposition only if $v_d \lesssim 0.01$\,cm\,s$^{-1}$.
  \item Deposition would remove most from the silent release, which stays close to the body and the seat, and would
  lower its doses further in the scenes of Section~\ref{sec:res-rooms}.
\end{itemize}

\emph{How dilute a passage arrives.} The panel test of ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009} implies a dilution of
$0.6$--$13\times10^{4}$ between release and nose, depending on the threshold set. The breath-averaged dilution of the
analytic model at the neighbour has a median of $1.8\times10^{3}$ (10--90\%: $2.5\times10^{2}$--$2.9\times10^{4}$).
The panel value is biased upwards, because it requires the least sensitive panellist to detect but divides by the
thresholds of the most sensitive. The analytic model is at the concentrated end of the panel range, as intended for
an upper bound, and within about an order of magnitude of it.

\emph{Composition and volume.} Larger passages contain more fermentation gas and less nitrogen (per passage,
$r = +0.56$ for \ce{CO2} and $-0.50$ for \ce{N2}, $n = 87$), while the sulfur concentrations do not change with volume
($|r| \le 0.22$, not significant); sulfur mass therefore rises with passage volume \cite{suarez1997}. At high
production rates almost all flatus is fermentation gas \cite{tomlin1991}. The fermentation-gas fraction rises with
output under bean, fibre and lactulose challenges in every study that measured both ($r = +0.86$ to $+0.99$, computed
from the published tables \cite{steggerda1966,steggerda1968,calloway1969,odonnell1984,marthinsen1982,hickey1972}; in one
person from 26\% at 55\,mL to 79\% at 720\,mL per 4\,h \cite{levitt1976}), whereas on an ordinary diet larger outputs
carry more nitrogen \cite{kirk1949}. The density of the gas does not follow the amount: carbon dioxide and hydrogen
rise together, and the mean molar mass, 22--32\,g\,mol$^{-1}$ across diets, is set by the substrate's \ce{CO2}:\ce{H2}
ratio (its correlation with output ranges from $-0.37$ to $+0.90$ across seven studies). Composition therefore cannot
link audibility to the amount of gas, since it enters audibility only through density (Section~\ref{sec:acoustics}).

\subsection{Most releases are physically silent}
\label{sec:res-acoustics}
In the lip-reed model, 85\% of the releases sampled never set the valve oscillating (Figure~\ref{fig:acoustics}); the
other 15\% are audible in any quiet room. No release in the sampled range oscillates without being heard in a quiet
room: the smallest oscillating release, about 33\,mL\,s$^{-1}$ through a 1\,mm orifice, gives a 47\,dB fundamental at
sofa distance (0.6\,m), against an 18\,dB hearing threshold at 160\,Hz. A release that is not heard is therefore either physically silent or masked by
background noise.

Masking needs loud backgrounds. In a restaurant or a subway car an oscillating release must exceed
0.36--0.60\,L\,s$^{-1}$ at 0.6\,m (1.8--3.0\,L\,s$^{-1}$ at 3\,m) to be heard, so only near-maximal passages are: 2--4\%
of all releases at a 160\,Hz fundamental, none at 100\,Hz and 6--13\% at 250--300\,Hz. Audible releases are fast, with a
median exit velocity of 90\,m\,s$^{-1}$ through a 1.5\,mm orifice.

\label{sec:res-ratio}

\emph{Equal mass.} At equal mass the audible release arrives pre-diluted by its own momentum
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:silencer}), and the silent one is stronger at the neighbour in 93\% of pairs. At the just-noticeable
ratio of 1.28, the silent release is perceptibly stronger in 59\% of pairs and indistinguishable in 39\%.

\emph{As emitted.} Audible passages are larger, and at the neighbour a silent passage is stronger in 48\% of pairs:
perceptibly stronger in 39\%, perceptibly weaker in 42\%. Trousers remove the jet momentum of both releases and lower
$P(R>1)$ to 0.31.

\emph{Which inputs matter.} As emitted, the two passage volumes account for about 0.6 of the variance of
$\log_{10} R$ at the neighbour; at equal mass, the eddy diffusivity $K$ accounts for 0.37. Sphincter pressure,
discharge coefficient, buoyancy and inhalation time each account for less than 0.5\%: the valve parameters set
whether a passage is heard, but hardly how strongly it smells.

\emph{Regime map} (Figure~\ref{fig:uq}a). The silent release is perceptibly stronger only at short travel times and
high Silencer numbers of the audible release. At equal mass even a barely audible release is perceptibly
weaker than a silent one, at the releaser's own nose (6.7\,s; $R = 1.61$ at the acoustic onset) and at the neighbour
(12.5\,s; $R = 1.33$).

\subsection{Real rooms: where the release goes}
\label{sec:res-rooms}
The analytic model carries every cloud straight to the nose. In the simulated rooms the warm air rising along the
body takes the silent release with it, while the audible jet leaves it, and the two then reach different noses.

\emph{Sofa.} The silent release rises in the gap between the releaser's back and the backrest, over the head and to
the ceiling (Figure~\ref{fig:planes}). Within 30\,s its breathing-zone OAV stays at or below $10^{-10}$ at both
noses. The audible jet hits the corner between seat and backrest, spreads sideways along the gap and reaches both
breathing zones at OAV 0.2--0.6. With the releaser reclined by 4.5\,\textdegree\ or leaning forward by
10\,\textdegree, the silent release again reaches neither breathing zone (at most $10^{-4}$;
Section~\ref{SI-sec:si-sofa}). After about 70\,s both clouds have returned from the ceiling, and at the neighbour the
two traces are indistinguishable (worst breath 0.39 for both), as Theorem~2 requires; at the releaser's own nose the
mixed silent cloud is 1.5--2 times higher and touches the trained-panel threshold for about a second near 100\,s.

\emph{Elevator.} In a closed car with four standing occupants, the result depends on what stands behind the
releaser. With a person behind (front row), the silent release rises in the 0.2\,m gap into that person's breathing
zone and is stronger at every nose ($R = 1.4$--$4.3$ at equal mass), reaching OAV 27 for the person behind. With the
car wall behind (back row), it rises between back and wall, and the audible release is stronger at every nose
($R = 0.20$--$0.55$). With a front-row releaser the person behind reaches threshold first (8.8\,s, against 17.6\,s at
the releaser's own nose), so the rule that whoever smells it first produced it holds for back-row releasers only.
Onlookers who see only who reacts identify the releaser at 0.28--0.52, against 0.25 by chance
(Section~\ref{SI-sec:si-elevator}).

\emph{Passages as emitted.} Scaling each scene's equal-mass doses by the volumes of passages as emitted
(Section~\ref{sec:uq}) gives Figure~\ref{fig:routing}. Silent passages are also smaller (median volume ratio 0.45),
which lowers the silent advantage where the flow favours the silent release: for the person behind a front-row
releaser, a silent passage is stronger in 35\% of pairs. Over the nine bystander noses $P(R>1)$ is 0--0.70, with an
equal-weight mean of 0.21, against 0.48 in the analytic model.

\subsection{Perception}
\label{sec:res-perception}
\emph{Breath sampling.} For a neighbour who is not warned, the worst breath still captures 81--99\% of the peak. A
listener warned by the sound needs about 25\,s of breath-hold for an even chance of missing the cloud
(Figure~\ref{SI-fig:breath}).

\emph{Silent versus audible.} Through Stevens' law, the median physical difference at the neighbour becomes a 1\%
difference in perceived intensity, below the olfactory just-noticeable difference of about 7\% in intensity.

\emph{Expectation.} An expectation effect would have to exceed 1\% in intensity to change which release seems
stronger. Measured label effects for brief exposures are 0--3\%, and their sign for an unwarned listener has not been
established \cite{herz2001,djordjevic2008,dalton1999}. We do not include expectation in the model.

\subsection{Memory}
\label{sec:res-memory}
Applying Theorem~3 to the simulated passages gives Table~\ref{tab:observers} and Figure~\ref{fig:memory}. Each base
rate $p_S$ assumes the cloud is centred on the neighbour's nose, so it is an upper bound.

The fewer passages are smelled, the further the remembered belief departs from the truth. When a sound makes the
bystander attentive, audible passages are smelled more often and the belief falls to 0.81; the truth for that
observer is also lower (0.32), because they do smell audible passages more often. The releaser, who notices every
release, shows no selection (Figure~\ref{fig:memory}a). In loud backgrounds only the largest passages are heard, and
this alone weakens the belief, so masking does not isolate the selection mechanism.
