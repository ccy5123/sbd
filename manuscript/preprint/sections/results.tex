\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The odorants and the gas that carries them}
\label{sec:res-composition}
Over two measured compositions and three threshold sets, methanethiol carries 43--94\% of the summed OAV and never
less than \ce{H2S} (Section~\ref{SI-sec:si-chem}). This differs from the conclusion drawn in
ref.~\citenum{suarez1998}, where \ce{H2S} is the main sulfur gas and the one whose concentration the judges' malodour
ratings followed. Weighted by odour thresholds, the same measured concentrations give methanethiol at least an equal
share: it is one fifth as concentrated, but its threshold is 5--6 times lower. Composition does not enter the
silent/audible comparison, because transport is linear and acts on all three gases alike. Stevens' law with these
OAVs predicts the judges' ratings of zinc-acetate-treated flatus (0.31--0.59 predicted, 0.28 and 0.59 rated) without
fitting.

Composition, not temperature, sets most of the buoyancy: after cooling to room temperature 25--38\% of passages are
heavier than air. Gas-phase oxidation removes at most 0.4\% of any odorant within 120\,s
(Table~\ref{SI-tab:reactivity}).

\subsection{Most releases are physically silent}
\label{sec:res-acoustics}
In the lip-reed model, 85\% of the releases sampled never set the valve oscillating (Figure~\ref{fig:acoustics}); the
other 15\% are audible in any quiet room. No release in the sampled range oscillates without being heard in a quiet
room: the smallest oscillating release, about 33\,mL\,s$^{-1}$ through a 1\,mm orifice, gives a 47\,dB fundamental at
sofa distance (0.6\,m), against an 18\,dB hearing threshold at 160\,Hz. A release that is not heard is therefore either physically silent or masked by
background noise.

Masking needs loud backgrounds: in a restaurant or a subway car only near-maximal passages are heard (0--13\% of
releases, depending on pitch). Audible releases are fast, with a median exit velocity of 90\,m\,s$^{-1}$.

\subsection{The ratio in the analytic model}
\label{sec:res-ratio}
The analytic model sends every cloud to the nose, so it bounds what dilution alone can do. Table~\ref{tab:ratio}
gives the breath-averaged ratio $R = C_\mathrm{silent}/C_\mathrm{audible}$ for 20\,000 random
pairs.

\begin{table}
\caption{Silent-to-audible concentration ratio in the analytic model (20\,000 pairs).}
\label{tab:ratio}
\begin{tabular}{llccc}
\toprule
receptor & basis & median $R$ & 5--95\% & $P(R>1)$ \\
\midrule
own nose & equal mass & 1.94 & 0.94--16.4 & 0.93 \\
own nose & as emitted & 1.23 & 0.20--6.1 & 0.59 \\
neighbour & equal mass & 1.45 & 0.97--10.3 & 0.93 \\
neighbour & as emitted & 0.96 & 0.15--4.8 & 0.48 \\
neighbour, denim & as emitted & 0.62 & 0.11--3.3 & 0.31 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\emph{Equal mass.} At equal mass the audible release arrives pre-diluted by its own momentum
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:silencer}), and the silent one is stronger at the neighbour in 93\% of pairs. At the just-noticeable
ratio of 1.28, the silent release is perceptibly stronger in 59\% of pairs and indistinguishable in 39\%.

\emph{As emitted.} Audible passages are larger, and at the neighbour a silent passage is stronger in 48\% of pairs:
perceptibly stronger in 39\%, perceptibly weaker in 42\%. Trousers remove the jet momentum of both releases and lower
$P(R>1)$ to 0.31.

\emph{Which inputs matter} (Figure~\ref{fig:uq}b). The two passage volumes explain about 0.6 of the variance of
$\log_{10} R$ as emitted; the valve parameters set whether a passage is heard, but hardly how strongly it smells.

\emph{Regime map} (Figure~\ref{fig:uq}a). The silent release is perceptibly stronger only at short travel times and
high Silencer numbers of the audible release. At equal mass even a barely audible release is perceptibly
weaker than a silent one, at the releaser's own nose (6.7\,s; $R = 1.61$ at the acoustic onset) and at the neighbour
(12.5\,s; $R = 1.33$).

\subsection{Real rooms: where the release goes}
\label{sec:res-rooms}
The analytic model carries every cloud straight to the nose. In the simulated rooms the warm air rising along the
body takes a slow release with it, while a fast jet leaves it, and the two then reach different noses.

\emph{Sofa.} The silent release rises in the gap between the releaser's back and the backrest, over the head and to
the ceiling (Figure~\ref{fig:planes}). At the neighbour, the worst breath over 150\,s (volumes as emitted) is 0.07
and 0.09 for the two silent releases and 0.16 and 16.9 for the audible ones; reclining or leaning forward moves the
silent values to 0.12--0.95, still below the trained-panel threshold. The dose rises with the ratio of the release's
momentum flux to that of the boundary layer it enters, $\Pi = M_0/m_\mathrm{p}$: a release must break out of the
boundary layer to reach a neighbour (Figure~\ref{fig:routing}a).

\emph{Standing pair.} Face to face and bare, the neighbour stays below OAV 0.2 for every release, in still air and
with ventilation, whatever $\Pi$: the body plumes carry both clouds upwards between the two people. Momentum is therefore
not the only variable; the arrangement of the bodies is the second.

\emph{Elevator.} In a closed car with a person behind the releaser, the silent release rises in the 0.2\,m gap into
that person's breathing zone: worst breath 260 for the median silent release, against 36 for the strongest audible
one. Every nose in the car passes OAV 1.

\emph{Releaser.} At the releaser's own nose the relation is not monotonic (Figure~\ref{fig:routing}b): the highest
doses at equal mass, up to OAV 70, come from releases with $\Pi \approx 10$--100, which leave the seat region but stay close to the
body.

\subsection{How often a silent passage is worse}
\label{sec:res-headline}
Table~\ref{tab:headline} gives the four outcomes for passages as emitted, weighted by how often people are in each
setting. For a bystander, nothing is smelled in 93\% of pairs (attentive) or 98\% (distracted). When something is,
the audible passage is worse about 13 times as often as the silent one: 6.9\% against 0.5\% for an attentive
bystander. The releaser smells more and still finds the audible passage worse four times as often. The elevator is
the exception: with a person behind the releaser, the silent passage is worse in 22\% of pairs and the audible one
in 20\%. It weighs little because people spend minutes a day in shared lifts, against hours on a sofa.

\emph{Robustness} (Section~\ref{SI-sec:si-robust}). The order of the two physical shares holds under every variation
we tried. Home- and office-centred days give 0.5 and 1.1\% silent worse against 8.4 and 3.9\% audible worse. The
psychometric tail (slope, logistic or floored shape, or a deterministic threshold) moves the ratio between 4 and 25.
In trousers the audible passage is worse more often (9.3 and 10.2\% when 70 and 85\% of passages are clothed; silent
worse 0.1\%). The large-eddy peak factors near threshold are 2.2 (1.0--6.6) for silent and 1.3 (0.27--64) for audible
releases; applied by class they give 1.2 against 8.2\%. A silent-only factor of about 14 would be needed to reverse
the order; only the adverse pairing of the highest silent with the lowest audible factor does so (3.6 against
2.3\%). Scaling every silent dose by the mesh-refinement factors (0.55 or 3.0) gives 0.3 or 1.6 against 6.8--6.9\%. The
source assumptions move the share of silent passages between 76 and 94\% without changing the order.

\subsection{Perception}
\label{sec:res-perception}
\emph{Breath sampling.} For a neighbour who is not warned, the worst breath still captures 81--99\% of the peak. A
listener warned by the sound needs about 25\,s of breath-hold for an even chance of missing the cloud
(Figure~\ref{SI-fig:breath}).

\emph{Expectation.} Measured label effects for brief exposures are 0--3\% in intensity, and their sign for an unwarned
listener has not been established \cite{herz2001,djordjevic2008,dalton1999}. We do not include expectation in the
model.

\subsection{Memory}
\label{sec:res-memory}
A bystander remembers a silent passage only if it was smelled. Among remembered pairs, the silent passage seems
worse in 88\% (attentive) and 97\% (distracted) of comparisons (Table~\ref{tab:headline}), while over all pairs it is
worse in 0.5 and 0.1\%. Among pairs where anything is smelled the truth is 6.9 and 9.1\%. The remembered share is not
fixed by definition: it falls as more passages are smelled and meets the truth, 29\%, when every passage is smelled
(Figure~\ref{fig:memory}). The releaser, who notices every release, shows no selection: memory equals the physical
shares, 19--24\% among pairs where anything is smelled, at any detection rate.
