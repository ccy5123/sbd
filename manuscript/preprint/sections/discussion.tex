\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the belief exists}
The physics we model does not make silent passages generally worse. At equal mass the silent advantage at a
neighbour is a median factor of 1.45, often below the just-noticeable ratio. As emitted, a silent passage is the
stronger one in 48\% of pairs in the analytic model and in \gap{v4; preprint: 21\% (0--70\%)} of bystander pairs in
the simulated scenes, where on a sofa it misses both noses on its first pass.

The belief is nevertheless documented across languages. Theorem~3 can explain it without any physical difference: a
passage enters memory if it is heard or smelled, so every remembered silent passage was smelled, whereas audible
passages are remembered whether or not they smelled. With literature observer models this selection turns a truth of
\gap{v4; preprint: 0.32--0.51} into a remembered belief of \gap{v4; preprint: 0.68--0.93}
(Table~\ref{tab:observers}). This shows that selection is sufficient for the belief, not that it is its cause; the
predictions below can decide. Documented memory biases act in the same direction: distinctive pairings are
over-remembered \cite{hamilton1976}, and judgements built from biased samples are rarely corrected
\cite{fiedler2000}. The phrase itself can also pass the belief on
without any experience \cite{chapman1967}; documentary evidence cannot separate the two routes
(Figure~\ref{SI-fig:culture}).

\subsection{Predictions}
\label{sec:predictions}
If selection is the cause, the belief should follow who notices what, and a survey can test this.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Releasers.} A releaser feels every release, so their noticing does not depend on smell and Theorem~3
  does not apply to their own passages. Releasers should hold the belief less firmly than bystanders. Their own
  passages also disgust them less: the item ``silent but smells strongly'' is rated 2.51 for one's own flatus and
  3.71 for a stranger's on a 1--5 scale \cite{liuzza2016}. This lowers the unpleasantness of their passages, not the
  contrast between silent and audible ones; only an expectation about their own odour \cite{hold1977} could carry
  the belief over to them.
  \item \emph{People without a sense of smell.} As bystanders they can notice a passage only by hearing it, so they
  never remember a silent passage. If they hold the belief, it came from the phrase, not from experience.
  \item \emph{Background noise.} Masking makes the quieter audible passages seem silent, so they enter memory only
  when smelled, but the passages still heard are the largest ones (Section~\ref{sec:res-memory}). The two act in
  opposite directions, so the effect of noise on the belief should be ambiguous and is not a clean test.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Where the odour comes from}
\label{sec:disc-chem}
Two facts the transport model takes as given have chemical explanations in the literature, which we did not test
(Section~\ref{SI-sec:si-origin}). The sulfur odorants are trace products held at a balance of production and fast
mucosal consumption, whereas passage volume is set by the bulk gases, which is why sulfur concentration and volume
are uncorrelated \cite{suarez1997}. Diet could shift this balance so that large passages carry less sulfur, in the
direction of the belief; no study has measured sulfur gases and flatus volume together across diets.

\subsection{When a silent release is worse}
At equal mass, at the releaser's own nose, in still air and within a few seconds, a silent release is perceptibly
stronger than a loud one (Figure~\ref{fig:uq}a). In the elevator, a silent release is also stronger for the person
standing behind a front-row releaser, who smells it first. In these two cases the saying holds.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Mean flow only.} The CFD resolves the mean flow; instantaneous peaks of a meandering cloud can exceed the
  mean by an order of magnitude. This can lift the sub-threshold audible doses on the sofa (OAV 0.2--0.6) above
  threshold, but not the silent ones ($10^{-10}$). In the forward-leaning posture the mean silent cloud passes within
  25\,cm of the neighbour's face, where a filament could reach the nose.
  \item \emph{Body plume.} The seated body plume is 40--56\% too fast, which shortens the travel times of the silent
  release by about 30\% in the seated case; the arrival times in the elevator (8.8 and 17.6\,s) are likely short by a
  similar amount. Standing plumes were not validated separately.
  \item \emph{Scenes and weights.} The as-emitted shares of Figure~\ref{fig:routing} weight the simulated scenes
  equally and assume the flow path does not depend on the passage volume. They describe these scenes, not how often
  each arises.
  \item \emph{Base rate.} The share of passages smelled has never been measured; the remembered belief of
  \gap{v4; preprint: 0.68--0.93} spans the observer models that bracket it.
  \item \emph{Valve state.} The 85\% silent share treats the onset pressure as a property of the tissue and does not
  distinguish flatus passing a relaxed sphincter from flatus passing a contracted one
  (Section~\ref{SI-sec:si-acoustics}).
\end{itemize}
