\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the belief exists}
Physics does not support the Silencer conjecture.
\begin{itemize}
  \item As emitted, a silent passage is stronger at a neighbour's nose in about half of all pairs.
  \item At equal mass its advantage is modest and often imperceptible.
  \item On a sofa it can miss both noses on its first pass, returning only once it is mixed into the room.
\end{itemize}
The belief is nevertheless held across cultures. Theorem~3 explains why. A passage enters memory if it is heard or
smelled, so every remembered silent passage was smelled, whereas audible passages are remembered whether or not
they smelled. At plausible base rates this selection alone turns a truth of 0.5 into a belief of 0.7--0.9
(Table~\ref{tab:observers}).

Known biases reinforce the selection.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A silent, smelly event is distinctive, and distinctive pairings are over-remembered \cite{hamilton1976}.
  \item Vivid episodes are easy to recall \cite{tversky1973}.
  \item Judgements built from such biased samples are rarely corrected \cite{fiedler2000,denrell2005}.
\end{itemize}

The phrase itself may also transmit the belief without any experience behind it \cite{chapman1967}, and this
competing route cannot be separated from experience with documentary evidence alone (Figure~\ref{fig:culture}).

\subsection{The emitter}
The emitter is the one observer the selection argument does not touch. They feel every release, so their noticing
does not depend on smell, and their comparison of silent and audible releases should follow physics. Three findings
bear on this.
\begin{itemize}
  \item People rate their own flatus as less disgusting than a stranger's. On a 1--5 scale the questionnaire item
  reads ``silent but smells strongly'', and it is rated 2.51 for one's own flatus and 3.71 for a stranger's
  \cite{liuzza2016,stevenson2005}. This lowers the absolute unpleasantness of the emitter's own releases, but not the
  contrast between silent and audible ones.
  \item The self-advantage appears even when people do not recognise their own odour \cite{mcburney1976}.
  \item Believing an odour is one's own shifts its hedonic rating \cite{hold1977}. Expectation is therefore the one
  route by which an emitter could share the belief.
\end{itemize}
No study has compared real own and other flatus, or asked whether emitters detect their own passages.

\subsection{Predictions}
The account makes three predictions, testable without new physics.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Emitters should hold the belief less firmly than bystanders.
  \item Anosmic people should never remember a silent passage at all.
  \item Effects of background noise should be ambiguous, because masking removes the quieter audible passages and
  so weakens the belief by itself.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{When a silent release really is worse}
At equal mass, at the releaser's own nose, in still air and within a few seconds, a silent release is perceptibly
stronger than a loud one (Figure~\ref{fig:uq}a). This corner of the regime map is where the folk saying is literally
true, and it belongs to the person least likely to report it.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Turbulence model.} The CFD is a RANS mean field. Real clouds meander, and instantaneous peaks can
  exceed the mean by an order of magnitude. This matters for sub-threshold audible values, not for silent values
  $10^{-10}$ below threshold.
  \item \emph{Validation bias.} The body plume is 40--56\% too fast.
  \item \emph{Coverage.} We simulated one release volume and two postures, with a 30\,s direct pass and, for one
  posture, a 120\,s window in a sealed room without supply air.
  \item \emph{No measurements on people.} We made no human measurements. The orifice diameter, release duration and
  valve threshold are assumptions, covered by the uncertainty analysis.
  \item \emph{Base rate.} The base rate of smelled passages has never been measured. We bracket it from population
  thresholds and attention.
  \item \emph{Composition.} All composition data come from one dataset of 16 volunteers on a bean-rich diet
  \cite{suarez1997,suarez1998}.
  \item \emph{Culture.} The cross-cultural evidence is documentary, not a prevalence survey.
\end{itemize}
