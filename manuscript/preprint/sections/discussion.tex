\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the belief exists}
The physics we model does not make silent passages generally worse. At equal mass the silent advantage at a
neighbour is a median factor of 1.45 in the analytic model, often below the just-noticeable ratio, and that model
sends every cloud to the nose. In the simulated rooms, with passages as emitted, a bystander on a sofa, where people
spend most of their shared time, finds the audible passage worse 18 times as often as the silent one, and this holds
when the uncertainties of mesh and peak factor are combined.

The belief is nevertheless documented across languages. Proposition~3 can explain it without any physical difference: a
passage enters memory if it is heard or smelled, so every remembered silent passage was smelled, whereas audible
passages are remembered whether or not they smelled. On the same simulated passages this selection turns a day-average truth of
0.5\% into a remembered 88\% (Table~\ref{tab:headline}), and the gap closes only as detection approaches certainty
(Figure~\ref{fig:memory}). This shows that selection is sufficient for the belief, not that it is its cause; the
predictions below can decide. Documented memory biases act in the same direction: distinctive pairings are
over-remembered \cite{hamilton1976}, and judgements built from biased samples are rarely corrected
\cite{fiedler2000}. A prior verbal association alone can make people report
co-occurrences that are not in what they saw \cite{chapman1967}, so the phrase itself may pass the belief on; documentary evidence cannot separate the two routes
(Figure~\ref{SI-fig:culture}). Attribution may add to both: a silent passage in company usually has no known source,
and a stranger's flatus disgusts more than one's own \cite{liuzza2016}.

\subsection{Predictions}
\label{sec:predictions}
If selection is the cause, the belief should follow who notices what, and a survey can test this.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Releasers.} A releaser feels every release, so their noticing does not depend on smell and Proposition~3
  does not apply to their own passages. Releasers should hold the belief less firmly than bystanders. Their own
  passages also disgust them less: the item ``silent but smells strongly'' is rated 2.51 for one's own flatus and
  3.71 for a stranger's on a 1--5 scale \cite{liuzza2016}. This lowers the unpleasantness of their passages, not the
  contrast between silent and audible ones; only an expectation about their own odour \cite{hold1977} could carry
  the belief over to them.
  \item \emph{People without a sense of smell.} As bystanders they can notice a passage only by hearing it, so they
  never remember a silent passage. If they hold the belief, it came from the phrase, not from experience.
  \item \emph{Background noise.} Masking makes quieter audible passages seem silent, so they enter memory only when
  smelled, while the passages still heard are the largest; the two effects oppose, so noise is not a clean test.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{When a silent release is worse}
At equal mass, at the releaser's own nose, in still air and within a few seconds, a silent release is perceptibly
stronger than a loud one (Figure~\ref{fig:uq}a). A passage deliberately slowed in company is such a case: the same
gas, released quietly. The source model does not include this behaviour, so in company silent passages may be larger
than as emitted. In a closed lift with someone standing behind the releaser, the slow
release rises straight into that person's breathing zone, and silent and audible passages are worse about equally
often (22 and 20\% of pairs). With the combined peak-factor and mesh uncertainties the silent passage is clearly worse there (47 against 14\%).
In these settings the saying holds; they are rare in a day.

\subsection{Scope}
\label{sec:scope}
The four rooms are representative rather than exhaustive, and the time spent in each is partly assumed, so the
outcomes are reported per setting and the day average follows the weights (Table~\ref{tab:headline}). The share of passages a bystander smells has never been
measured, so the remembered share is reported against it (Figure~\ref{fig:memory}). The flow path of a passage is
taken to depend on its momentum and not on its volume. The simulated boundary layer is too fast, the intake fraction
1.5 times the measured one and the releaser's dose mesh-dependent (factor 3.0; Section~\ref{sec:cfd}), and the order of releases in a run
changes the dose by a factor 0.4 at the neighbour and 0.8 at the releaser (Section~\ref{SI-sec:si-validation}); none comes
near the silent-only factor of 16 that would reverse the order on the sofa (Section~\ref{sec:res-headline}).
