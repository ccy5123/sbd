\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Routing.} The body's convective boundary layer takes a slow release and lets a fast one pass, so the
  momentum of a release next to a body decides which nose it reaches. On a sofa the silent release rises behind the
  releaser to the ceiling; between two standing people both releases rise between them; in a closed lift the silent
  release goes straight to the person standing behind the releaser.
  \item \emph{Physics says it depends.} While two releases of the same gas are carried passively by the same flow, the
  slower one never gives a higher peak. With passages as emitted at physiological driving pressures, which passage is
  stronger when both are smelled depends on where the noses are: on a sofa the audible one, twice as often as the
  silent one but within the numerical uncertainty; in a closed lift, for the person behind the releaser, the silent
  one (55 against 33\% of pairs), in every variation tested, while for those beside the releaser it is the audible
  one. Between standing people either is rarely smelled, and only at the
  room level.
  \item \emph{Memory says mostly.} A silent passage is noticed only if it is smelled, and this selection alone can
  explain the belief: among remembered pairs the silent passage seems worse in 95\% (attentive) to 99\%
  (distracted) of comparisons. The explanation predicts that releasers hold the belief less firmly than bystanders
  and that people without a sense of smell never remember a silent passage.
\end{itemize}
