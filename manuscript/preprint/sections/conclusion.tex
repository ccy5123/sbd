\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Routing.} The body's convective boundary layer takes a slow release and lets a fast one pass, so the
  momentum of a release next to a body decides which nose it reaches. On a sofa the silent release rises behind the
  releaser to the ceiling; between two standing people both releases rise between them; in a closed lift the silent
  release goes straight to the person standing behind the releaser.
  \item \emph{Physics says seldom.} While two releases of the same gas are carried passively by the same flow, the
  slower one never gives a higher peak. With passages as emitted at physiological driving pressures, a bystander on
  a sofa finds the audible passage worse about seven times as often as the silent one (4.1 against 0.55\% of pairs),
  and the order survives the mesh and peak-factor uncertainties combined at their median values. Between standing people neither is smelled; only in a
  closed lift is the silent passage worse as often or more.
  \item \emph{Memory says mostly.} A silent passage is noticed only if it is smelled, and this selection alone can
  explain the belief: among remembered pairs the silent passage seems worse in 91\% (attentive) to 98\%
  (distracted) of comparisons. The explanation predicts that releasers hold the belief less firmly than bystanders
  and that people without a sense of smell never remember a silent passage.
\end{itemize}
