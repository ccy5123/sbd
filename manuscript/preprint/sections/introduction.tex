\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% Hook: paraphrase only; no quotation of the verse, no image of the character.
In the film \emph{3 Idiots} \cite{hirani2009}, the student Chatur Ramalingam is nicknamed ``Silencer'': the
memory-enhancing pills he takes give him silent but foul flatulence. In one scene he recites a mock-Sanskrit verse
that ranks flatus by its sound and declares the silent release the deadliest. The belief behind the joke is old. An
1811 slang dictionary defines a related term as an escape ``more obvious to the nose than ears'' \cite{grose1811},
and English has called such releases ``silent but deadly'' since at least 1970.

A documentary survey of 32 languages (Section~\ref{SI-sec:si-culture}) finds the distinction between silent and loud
releases lexicalised in at least five language families. The claim that the silent release smells worse is stated
explicitly in Chinese and English sayings, and it is built into the definitions of smell-specific ``silent'' words
that arose independently at least four times within Indo-European. We found no test of the claim.

Both halves of the claim are ambiguous.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A release can be silent because it never sets the anal valve oscillating, or because background noise
  masks it.
  \item ``Deadlier'' can mean a higher peak at a nose, a larger total dose, or a stronger memory.
  \item The comparison can be between the same gas released in two ways (equal mass), between passages as they are
  actually emitted, or between the passages an observer happens to notice.
\end{itemize}
These bases give different answers, and we keep them apart throughout (Section~\ref{sec:deadliness}).

We follow one passage from its chemistry through sound and dispersion to memory (Figure~\ref{SI-fig:chain}).
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Chemistry: which odorant carries the smell, what sets the buoyancy of a passage, and whether the odorants
  survive the trip to a nose.
  \item Sound: a lip-reed model of the valve gives the share of releases that are physically silent.
  \item Dispersion: two theorems bound what linear transport can do to the silent/audible ratio; an analytic model of
  starting jets and puffs, checked against canonical jets and body plumes, gives the ratio over the range of
  releases; and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) of two people on a sofa, four in an elevator and two standing face
  to face shows where the body's own airflow takes each release.
  \item Memory: a third theorem and literature observer models give how often a remembered silent passage seems the
  stronger one.
\end{enumerate}
