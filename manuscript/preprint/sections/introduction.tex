\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The belief that a silent passage of flatus smells worse than an audible one is old. An 1811 slang dictionary
defines a related term as an escape ``more obvious to the nose than ears'' \cite{grose1811}, and English has called
such releases ``silent but deadly'' since at least 1970 \cite{green2026}. A documentary survey of 32 languages
(Section~\ref{SI-sec:si-culture}) finds the distinction between silent and loud releases lexicalised in at least five
language families. The claim that the silent release smells worse is stated explicitly in Chinese and English sayings,
and it is built into the definitions of smell-specific ``silent'' words that arose independently at least four times
within Indo-European.
% Film: paraphrase only; no quotation of the verse, no image of the character.
It is still current: in the film \emph{3 Idiots} \cite{hirani2009}, a student, Chatur, nicknamed ``Silencer'',
declares the silent release the deadliest.

We found no test of the claim. The odour of flatus is well characterised: hydrogen sulfide, methanethiol and
dimethyl sulfide carry most of it \cite{suarez1998,tangerman2009}, and the volume of a passage does not predict its sulfur
content \cite{suarez1997}. Its sound has been attributed to vibration of the anal skin, like the buzzing lips of a
brass player \cite{ancalle2021}, but no peer-reviewed study has measured it. The only data relating sound to smell
are informal and not peer reviewed: in 1\,619 recorded passages, louder ones were very slightly smellier by a gas-sensor
reading ($R^2 = 0.016$), and none was silent \cite{snyder2020}. Their direction agrees with what we find, but they
cannot test a claim about silent passages and human noses.

The question is narrow, but answering it needs three results that are not specific to flatus.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Where does a release next to a body go?} The warm convective boundary layer around a person rises
  along the body to the breathing zone \cite{licina2014,licina2015} and carries pollutants released near the body
  with it \cite{rim2009}. Source location governs exposure when room air mixes poorly \cite{rim2010}; a
  source at a seated person's groin gives a breathing-zone intake fraction of 5--10 per mille \cite{licina2017}. CFD can follow particles shed from skin and clothing in this flow \cite{alassaad2021}. These studies concern passive
  sources and the occupant's own exposure. We show that the momentum of a release decides whether the boundary layer
  takes it, and so which of several nearby noses it reaches.
  \item \emph{Can a slower release give a higher peak?} We prove that it cannot while both releases are carried
  passively by the same flow, whatever that flow is. A slower release can win only near the source: by escaping
  the pre-dilution of a fast jet, by at most a velocity ratio, or by being carried to a different nose.
  \item \emph{How can a sensory belief be held more firmly than the physics supports?} When an event can be noticed
  through either of two senses, selecting the noticed events makes the two senses appear related (Berkson's bias
  \cite{berkson1946,snoep2014}). We show that this selection alone can produce the belief even where silent and
  audible passages are equally strong, and we derive predictions that can test this explanation.
\end{itemize}

Both halves of the claim are also ambiguous. A release can be silent because it never sets the anal valve
oscillating, or because background noise masks it. ``Deadlier'' can mean a higher peak at a nose, a larger total
dose, or a stronger memory. The comparison can be between the same gas released in two ways (equal mass), between
passages as they are actually emitted, or between the passages an observer happens to notice. These bases give
different answers, and we keep them apart throughout (Section~\ref{sec:deadliness}).

We follow one passage from its chemistry through sound and dispersion to memory (Figure~\ref{fig:chain}).
Chemistry fixes which odorant carries the smell, what sets the buoyancy of a passage, and whether the odorants survive
the trip to a nose. A lip-reed model of the valve gives the share of releases that are physically silent. Two
propositions bound what linear transport can do to the silent/audible ratio; an analytic model of starting jets and
puffs, checked against canonical jets and body plumes, gives the ratio over the range of releases; and computational
fluid dynamics (CFD) of two people on a sofa, four in a lift and two standing face to face shows where the body's
own airflow takes each release. A third proposition and literature models of observers give how often a remembered silent
passage seems the stronger one.
