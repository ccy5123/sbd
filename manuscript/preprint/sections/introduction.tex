\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% Hook (PLAN.md section 2): paraphrase only; no quotation of the verse, no image of the character.
In the film \emph{3 Idiots} \cite{hirani2009}, the student Chatur Ramalingam is nicknamed ``Silencer'' because his
releases are inaudible but foul. A mock-Sanskrit verse in the film ranks flatus by its sound, descending from a loud,
vehicle-like blast to the silent release, which it declares the deadliest. The verse is a joke, but the belief behind
it is not new. An 1811 slang dictionary defines a related term as an escape ``more obvious to the nose than ears''
\cite{grose1811}, and English has called such releases ``silent but deadly'' since at least 1970.

The belief is widespread. A documentary survey of 32 languages finds the following \gap{cite survey data archive}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the distinction between silent and loud releases is lexicalised in at least five language families;
  \item the stronger claim, that the silent release smells worse, is stated explicitly in Chinese and English sayings;
  \item it is also encoded in the definitions of smell-specific ``silent'' words that arose independently at least
  four times within Indo-European (Figure~\ref{fig:culture}).
\end{itemize}
We call the belief the \emph{Silencer conjecture}: the less audible the release, the more offensive the odour. As far
as we know, it has never been tested.

The conjecture is less simple than it sounds, because ``silent'' and ``deadly'' are both ambiguous.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A release can be silent because it never sets the anal valve into oscillation, or because background noise
  masks it.
  \item ``Deadlier'' can mean a stronger peak at a nose, a larger total dose, or simply a stronger memory.
  \item The comparison can be made between the same gas released in two ways (equal mass), between passages as they
  are actually emitted, or between the passages an observer happens to notice.
\end{itemize}
These three bases give different answers, and we keep them apart throughout (Section~\ref{sec:deadliness}).

We follow one passage from sound to memory and make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A mechanistic chain: lip-reed acoustics decide which releases are heard; starting-jet and puff dispersion,
  checked against canonical jets and body plumes and against computational fluid dynamics of two people on a sofa,
  decide what reaches a nose; breath-resolved perception decides what is noticed.
  \item Two transport theorems that bound what physics can do: in the linear regime a quieter release of the same
  gas can never produce a higher peak. The only exception, near-field pre-dilution of a loud jet, is governed by a
  single velocity ratio, which we call the Silencer number.
  \item A selection theorem: because a silent passage is noticed only if it is smelled, memory alone produces the
  belief from a coin-flip truth.
  \item A cross-cultural survey of the belief.
\end{enumerate}
