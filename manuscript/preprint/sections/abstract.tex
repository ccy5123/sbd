The belief that a silent passage of flatus smells worse than an audible one (the ``Silencer conjecture'') is found
across many languages but has never been tested. We follow one passage from sound to memory.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Sound.} Lip-reed acoustics decide which releases are heard; 79\% are physically silent.
  \item \emph{Dispersion.} Starting-jet and puff dispersion, validated against canonical jets and body plumes, and
  computational fluid dynamics of two people on a sofa decide what reaches a nose.
  \item \emph{Perception.} Breath-resolved perception decides what is noticed.
  \item \emph{Memory.} A selection model decides what is remembered.
\end{itemize}
We prove that in the linear transport regime a quieter release of the same gas can never produce a higher peak. The
only exception is near-field pre-dilution of loud jets, governed by a velocity ratio we call the Silencer number.

Per molecule, a silent release is delivered somewhat more efficiently (median ratio 1.35 at a neighbour), often below
the olfactory just-noticeable difference. As passages are actually emitted, a silent one is stronger at the
neighbour's nose in 47\% of pairs. On a sofa, the body's convective boundary layer carries a silent release past both
noses on its first pass, in two postures, while the audible jet reaches them; after a minute of room recirculation,
the two become indistinguishable at the neighbour's nose.

A bystander nevertheless remembers silent passages as worse in 67--92\% of comparisons, because a silent passage
enters memory only if it was smelled. An emitter, who notices every release, has no such bias.
