The belief that a silent passage of flatus smells worse than an audible one appears in many languages but is untested.
We follow a passage from sound through dispersion to a bystander's memory, with a lip-reed model of the anal valve and
literature dispersion and observer models. Between 55 and 85\% of releases, depending on the source assumed, never set the valve oscillating. We prove that a
slower release of the same gas cannot give a higher peak while both are carried passively by the same flow. In flow
simulations of seated and standing people, the warm air rising along the body takes a slow release and lets a fast one
pass, and so decides whose nose a release reaches. At physiological driving pressures, a bystander on a sofa smells
audible passages more often and, when both are smelled, finds the audible one stronger twice as often as the silent
one; in a closed lift the slow release rises into the breathing zone of the person behind, for whom the silent one is
stronger more often (55 against 33\% of pairs). Which is worse thus depends on where the noses are, and the sofa
comparison lies within the combined numerical uncertainty. A silent passage is noticed only if smelled, and this
selection alone can explain the belief: among remembered pairs the silent passage seems worse in 95\%. It predicts that
releasers believe it less firmly than bystanders and that anosmic people never remember a silent passage.
