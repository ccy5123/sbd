The belief that a silent passage of flatus smells worse than an audible one (the ``Silencer conjecture'') is
documented across languages but has never been tested mechanistically.
We follow one passage from sound to memory: lip-reed acoustics decide which releases are heard; starting-jet and puff
dispersion, checked against canonical jet and body-plume data and computational fluid dynamics of a seated pair, decide
what reaches a nose; breath-resolved perception decides what is noticed; and a selection model decides what is
remembered.
Per molecule, a silent release is delivered somewhat more efficiently (median ratio 1.35 at a neighbour), mostly below
the olfactory just-noticeable difference.
As passages are actually emitted, a silent one is stronger at the neighbour's nose in 47\% of pairs.
A bystander nevertheless remembers silent passages as worse in 73--97\% of comparisons, because a silent passage enters
memory only if it was smelled.
An emitter, who notices every release, has no such bias.
