The belief that a silent passage of flatus smells worse than an audible one appears in many languages but has not
been tested. We follow a passage from its chemistry through sound and dispersion to a bystander's memory, combining
measured odorant data, a lip-reed model of the anal valve, analytic and computational models of dispersion, and
models of what an observer notices.

Methanethiol carries 43--94\% of the summed odour activity of the three sulfur odorants, never less than hydrogen
sulfide. Composition, not temperature, sets whether a passage rises or sinks, and gas-phase oxidation removes at most
0.4\% of any odorant within two minutes in an office, so the odorants travel unchanged with the air. In the
lip-reed model, 85\% of releases in the measured range of passage volumes never set the valve oscillating; they are
physically silent rather than quiet.

We prove that while transport is linear in concentration, a quieter release of the same gas cannot produce a higher
peak concentration. Near the source, a loud jet is diluted before it slows to the speed of the surrounding air, by a
factor proportional to the ratio of exit to ambient velocity, which we call the Silencer number. At equal mass this
pre-dilution lets a silent release reach a neighbour's nose at a median 1.45 times the concentration of an audible
one, often below the just-noticeable ratio of 1.28. Silent passages are also smaller, and as emitted a silent
passage is the stronger one at the neighbour in 48\% of pairs.

In computational fluid dynamics of real scenes, the warm air rising along the body carries a release to different
noses depending on its momentum. On a sofa the silent release rises behind the releaser's back to the ceiling and
reaches neither nose within 30\,s, while the audible one reaches both. In an elevator the silent release is stronger
at every nose when a person stands behind the releaser and weaker at every nose when a wall does. Across these scenes
a silent passage as emitted is the stronger one at a bystander's nose in 0--70\% of pairs (mean 21\%).

A silent passage can be noticed only by smell, so every silent passage a bystander remembers was smelled, while
audible passages are remembered whether or not they were. With literature models of untrained observers, a
remembered silent passage seems the stronger one in 68--93\% of comparisons, against 32--51\% over all passages. The
releaser, who notices every release, is not subject to this selection.
