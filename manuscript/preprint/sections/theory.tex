\section{Definitions and theory}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{What ``deadlier'' means}
\label{sec:deadliness}
A passage is a release of volume $V$ over a duration $T$ through an effective orifice of diameter $d$, with exit
velocity $u_0 = 4V/(\pi d^2 T)$. We take its \emph{deadliness} for a given person to be the perceived intensity of the
worst single inhalation,
\begin{equation}
  I = k\,\mathrm{OAV}_\mathrm{b}^{\,n},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{OAV}_\mathrm{b} = \max_{\text{breaths}}
  \frac{\int_\mathrm{insp} \dot V_\mathrm{insp}(t)\,\mathrm{OAV}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}{\int_\mathrm{insp} \dot V_\mathrm{insp}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t},
  \label{eq:deadliness}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{OAV} = \sum_i c_i/c_{\mathrm{th},i}$ is the summed odour activity value of hydrogen sulfide,
methanethiol and dimethyl sulfide at the nose and $n$ is the Stevens exponent.

The breath is the natural unit for three reasons.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Perceived intensity saturates within a single natural sniff: one sniff is rated like seven, and the optimum
  lies between 0.4 and 0.6\,s \cite{laing1983,laing1985}.
  \item Within that window intensity still integrates: longer \ce{H2S} pulses of 100--300\,ms are rated stronger
  \cite{frasnelli2006}.
  \item The sniff is the unit of olfactory processing \cite{uchida2003,kepecs2006}, and regulatory odour-impact
  assessment also works with per-breath peaks rather than hourly means \cite{schauberger2012}.
\end{itemize}
Cumulative exposure is reported as a secondary metric.

Every comparison below states its basis:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[(A)] \emph{equal mass}: the same gas, released quietly or loudly;
  \item[(B)] \emph{as emitted}: silent and audible passages drawn from their own volume distributions;
  \item[(C)] \emph{as remembered}: only the passages an observer noticed.
\end{itemize}
Folk experience is basis (C). Physics answers (A) and (B).

\subsection{Linear transport bounds the silent advantage}
\label{sec:thm1}
Odorants at sub-ppm levels are a passive scalar. Once the release has lost its own momentum and buoyancy, the
concentration at a fixed nose is a linear functional of the release history $q(t)$ (volume of flatus per unit time):
\begin{equation}
  C(t) = \int G(t-s)\,q(s)\,\mathrm{d}s = (G * q)(t), \qquad \int q\,\mathrm{d}s = V,
  \label{eq:green}
\end{equation}
where the Green's function $G$ encodes everything about the room, the bodies, the furniture and the ventilation. Time
invariance of $G$ requires the flow to be statistically stationary over the release, which holds for the
quasi-steady convective boundary layers and room flows considered here.

\paragraph{Theorem 1.}
Let a silent release be a smoothed version of an audible one, $q_\mathrm{s} = q_\mathrm{a} * \kappa$ with
$\kappa \ge 0$ and $\int \kappa = 1$. Then, for any $G$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] the time-integrated dose $\int C\,\mathrm{d}t = V\int G$ is the same for both;
  \item[(ii)] the peak concentration of the silent release does not exceed that of the audible one.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Proof.} (i) follows from integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:green} over $t$. For (ii),
$C_\mathrm{s} = G*q_\mathrm{a}*\kappa = C_\mathrm{a}*\kappa$, and Young's inequality gives
$\|C_\mathrm{s}\|_\infty \le \|C_\mathrm{a}\|_\infty\,\|\kappa\|_1 = \|C_\mathrm{a}\|_\infty$. \hfill$\square$

A top-hat release lasting $nT$ is a top-hat lasting $T$ convolved with
$\kappa = n^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\delta(t-jT)$, so stretching a release by an integer factor is covered. The same
argument applies to the breath-averaged OAV of Eq.~\eqref{eq:deadliness}, which is itself a convolution of $C$ with a
non-negative kernel. In the linear regime, therefore, a quiet release can never be deadlier than a loud release of the
same gas, whatever the posture, room or flow.

\subsection{Release mode is forgotten}
\label{sec:thm2}
\paragraph{Theorem 2.}
In a closed, mixed room of fluid volume $V_\mathrm{room}$, $G(t)\to 1/V_\mathrm{room}$ as $t\to\infty$. Both release
modes therefore approach $C = V/V_\mathrm{room}$, and their ratio tends to one. More generally, any difference between
release modes is carried by the early, near-field part of $G$, and it decays with travel time and distance.

\subsection{Where the theorems fail: momentum and buoyancy}
\label{sec:nonlinear}
Equation~\eqref{eq:green} fails only near the source, where the release still carries its own momentum and buoyancy and
so changes the flow that carries it. Two effects survive this.

\emph{Near-field pre-dilution.} A momentum-dominated release entrains ambient air until its self-induced velocity
has decayed to the ambient velocity $u_h$ (the body's convective boundary layer or room air). For a starting jet, a
puff or a steady jet alike, the dilution at that hand-off is
\begin{equation}
  D_1 \approx \beta\,\frac{u_0}{u_h} \equiv \beta\,\mathrm{Si}, \qquad \beta = O(1),
  \label{eq:silencer}
\end{equation}
because entrained mass is inversely proportional to the remaining velocity (Section~\ref{sec:methods}; jet and puff
coefficients from refs.~\citenum{sangras2002,diez2003}). We call $\mathrm{Si} = u_0/u_h$ the \emph{Silencer number}. A
loud release ($\mathrm{Si}\approx 60$) arrives pre-diluted; a quiet one ($\mathrm{Si}\approx 5$) does not. This is the
only mechanism by which, at equal mass, a silent release can deliver a higher concentration, and it is bounded by the
ratio of the two Silencer numbers.

\emph{Routing.} Momentum also decides which flow the release joins, and different flows lead to different noses.
This effect can take either sign; the sofa simulations of Section~\ref{sec:results} show how large it can be.

\subsection{Selection makes the belief}
\label{sec:thm3}
Let $H$ be the event that a passage is heard and $S$ the event that it is smelled. An observer can only compare
passages they noticed, $N = H\cup S$.

\paragraph{Theorem 3.}
Whatever the joint law of $H$ and $S$,
\begin{equation}
  P(S \mid \lnot H, N) = 1, \qquad P(S \mid H, N) = P(S \mid H).
  \label{eq:berkson}
\end{equation}

\emph{Proof.} On $\lnot H$, noticing requires smelling, so $\lnot H\cap N = \lnot H \cap S$. On $H$, $N$ is certain, so
conditioning on $N$ adds nothing. \hfill$\square$

Among remembered passages, then, every silent one stank, while audible ones stank only at the base rate $p_S$, even
when sound and smell are independent. The gap $1-P(S\mid H)$ is largest when smelly passages are rare. This is the
deterministic ``OR-collider'' case of Berkson's bias \cite{berkson1946,snoep2014}.

An observer whose noticing does not depend on smell has no such bias. The emitter, who feels every release, has
$N$ certain for all passages and so compares silent and audible releases at their true frequencies.
