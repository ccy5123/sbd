\section{Definitions and theory}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{What ``deadlier'' means}
\label{sec:deadliness}
A passage is a release of volume $V$ over a duration $T$ through an effective orifice of diameter $d$, with exit
velocity $u_0 = 4V/(\pi d^2 T)$. We take its \emph{deadliness} for a given person to be the perceived intensity of the
worst single inhalation,
\begin{equation}
  I = k\,\mathrm{OAV}_\mathrm{b}^{\,n},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{OAV}_\mathrm{b} = \max_{\text{breaths}}
  \frac{\int_\mathrm{insp} \dot V_\mathrm{insp}(t)\,\mathrm{OAV}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}{\int_\mathrm{insp} \dot V_\mathrm{insp}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t},
  \label{eq:deadliness}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{OAV} = \sum_i c_i/c_{\mathrm{th},i}$ is the summed odour activity value of hydrogen sulfide,
methanethiol and dimethyl sulfide at the nose and $n$ is the Stevens exponent. The breath is the natural unit:
perceived intensity saturates within one natural sniff \cite{laing1983}, still integrates within it
\cite{frasnelli2006}, and the sniff is the unit of olfactory processing \cite{kepecs2006}. Cumulative exposure is
reported as a secondary metric.

Every comparison below states its basis:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[(A)] \emph{equal mass}: the same gas, released quietly or loudly;
  \item[(B)] \emph{as emitted}: silent and audible passages drawn from their own volume distributions;
  \item[(C)] \emph{as remembered}: only the passages an observer noticed.
\end{itemize}
Folk experience is basis (C). Physics answers (A) and (B).

\subsection{Linear transport bounds the silent advantage}
\label{sec:thm1}
Odorants at sub-ppm levels are a passive scalar: the air carries them without being affected by them. Once the
release has lost its own momentum and buoyancy, the concentration at a fixed nose is a linear functional of the
release history $q(t)$ (volume of flatus per unit time):
\begin{equation}
  C(t) = \int G(t-s)\,q(s)\,\mathrm{d}s = (G * q)(t), \qquad \int q\,\mathrm{d}s = V,
  \label{eq:green}
\end{equation}
where the Green's function $G$ encodes everything about the room, the bodies, the furniture and the ventilation.
Time invariance of $G$ requires the flow to be statistically stationary over the release, which holds for the
quasi-steady convective boundary layers and room flows considered here.

\paragraph{Theorem 1.}
Let a silent release be a smoothed version of an audible one, $q_\mathrm{s} = q_\mathrm{a} * \kappa$ with
$\kappa \ge 0$ and $\int \kappa = 1$. Then, for any $G$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] the time-integrated dose $\int C\,\mathrm{d}t = V\int G$ is the same for both;
  \item[(ii)] the peak concentration of the silent release does not exceed that of the audible one.
\end{enumerate}
The proof (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-proofs}) needs nothing about $G$ beyond linearity, and it covers the
breath-averaged OAV of Eq.~\eqref{eq:deadliness} and releases stretched by any integer factor. In the linear regime,
therefore, a quiet release can never be deadlier than a loud release of the same gas, whatever the posture, room or
flow.

\subsection{Release mode is forgotten}
\label{sec:thm2}
\paragraph{Theorem 2.}
In a closed, mixed room of fluid volume $V_\mathrm{room}$, $G(t)\to 1/V_\mathrm{room}$ as $t\to\infty$. Both release
modes therefore approach $C = V/V_\mathrm{room}$, and their ratio tends to one. More generally, any difference between
release modes is carried by the early, near-field part of $G$, and it decays with travel time and distance.

\subsection{Where the theorems fail: momentum and buoyancy}
\label{sec:nonlinear}
Equation~\eqref{eq:green} fails only near the source, where the release still carries its own momentum and buoyancy
and so changes the flow that carries it. Two effects survive this.

\emph{Near-field pre-dilution.} A momentum-dominated release entrains ambient air until its self-induced velocity
has decayed to the ambient velocity $u_h$ (the body's convective boundary layer or room air). For a starting jet, a
puff or a steady jet alike, the dilution at that hand-off is
\begin{equation}
  D_1 \approx \beta\,\frac{u_0}{u_h} \equiv \beta\,\mathrm{Si}, \qquad \beta = O(1)
  \label{eq:silencer}
\end{equation}
(Appendix~\ref{sec:app-nearfield}). We call $\mathrm{Si} = u_0/u_h$ the \emph{Silencer number}. An audible release
(in the analytic model $\mathrm{Si} > 280$) arrives pre-diluted; a silent one ($\mathrm{Si}\approx 5$) does not. At
equal mass, pre-dilution favours the silent release by at most the ratio of the two Silencer numbers.

\emph{Routing.} Momentum also sets which flow the release joins, and different flows lead to different noses. This
effect can favour either release. In the rooms of Section~\ref{sec:res-rooms} it changes the ratio by more than
pre-dilution does.

\subsection{Selection biases what is remembered}
\label{sec:thm3}
Let $H$ be the event that a passage is heard and $S$ the event that it is smelled. An observer can only compare
passages they noticed, $N = H\cup S$.

\paragraph{Theorem 3.}
Whatever the joint law of $H$ and $S$,
\begin{equation}
  P(S \mid \lnot H, N) = 1, \qquad P(S \mid H, N) = P(S \mid H).
  \label{eq:berkson}
\end{equation}
The proof is two lines (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-proofs}). Among remembered passages, then, every silent one stank, while
audible ones stank only at the base rate $p_S$, even when sound and smell are independent. The gap $1-P(S\mid H)$ is
largest when smelly passages are rare. This is Berkson's bias \cite{berkson1946,snoep2014}: selecting on an event that
either of two causes can produce (here, noticing, produced by hearing or by smelling) makes the two causes appear
related among the selected cases. Nothing in it is specific to flatus: any event noticed through either of two senses
is remembered with this bias.

An observer whose noticing does not depend on smell has no such bias. The emitter, who feels every release, has
$N$ certain for all passages and so compares silent and audible releases at their true frequencies.
