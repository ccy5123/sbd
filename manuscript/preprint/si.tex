\section{Odorant chemistry}
\label{sec:si-chem}
\subsection{Methods}
\emph{Odour activity.} Each sulfur gas contributes an odour activity value (OAV), its mole fraction over its detection
threshold, $\mathrm{OAV}_i = x_i/\theta_i$. The passage's OAV is their sum; we assume additivity and do not model
mixture suppression. We compare two measured compositions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item 87 passages of 16 volunteers \cite{suarez1998}, used throughout the dispersion model;
  \item 28 passages measured by gas chromatography: \ce{H2S} 149, methanethiol 216 and dimethyl sulfide
  144\,nmol\,L$^{-1}$ \cite{tangerman2009}.
\end{itemize}
Three threshold sets bracket the observer: a trained panel \cite{nagata2003}, population geometric means
\cite{amoore1983,wilby1969} and the lowest values in the literature, as compiled in ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009}.

\emph{Removal check.} Zinc acetate precipitates \ce{H2S} and thiols; activated charcoal adsorbs all three gases. For both
treatments, ref.~\citenum{suarez1998} reports the gas concentrations and two judges' ratings before and after. We
predict the rating ratio as $(\mathrm{OAV}_\mathrm{after}/\mathrm{OAV}_\mathrm{before})^n$ with $n = 0.20$--$0.45$
\cite{collins2001}, without fitting.

\emph{Buoyancy.} The reduced gravity $g' = g(\rho_a-\rho_g)/\rho_a$ of each of 16 measured compositions
\cite{suarez1997} is split into a thermal part, $g(1-T_a/T_g)$, for gas at 37\,\textdegree C in air at
22\,\textdegree C, and a compositional part, $g(M_a-M_g)/M_a$, at equal temperature with air at 50\% relative
humidity. The water-vapour content of flatus has not been measured, so we bracket dry and saturated gas.

\emph{Gas-phase and surface losses.} Gas-phase losses use IUPAC rate constants at 298\,K for reaction with OH,
\ce{O3}, \ce{NO3} and Cl \cite{atkinson2004}, with oxidant levels measured in an office: OH
$0.6$--$9.8\times10^{5}$\,cm$^{-3}$ and \ce{O3} 0.7--2.6\,ppb \cite{fiorentino2023}. Two assumed upper cases add OH
$10^6$\,cm$^{-3}$ with \ce{O3} 40\,ppb, and Cl $10^6$\,cm$^{-3}$ during hypochlorite cleaning. No deposition velocity
$v_d$ has been published for these gases on indoor surfaces, so surface loss is bracketed as $k = v_d\,S/V$ with
$S/V = 3$\,m$^{-1}$.

\emph{Dilution in a panel test.} Ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009} reports the lowest source concentration that every panel
member detected when 100\,mL was released below the nose. Divided by a detection threshold, it gives the dilution
between release and nose in that test, which we compare with the breath-averaged dilution of the analytic model.

\subsection{Results}
\emph{Which molecule.} Over the two measured compositions and three threshold sets, methanethiol carries 43--94\% of
the summed OAV and never less than \ce{H2S} (Figure~\ref{fig:chem}a).
\begin{itemize}
  \item With the composition of ref.~\citenum{suarez1998}, methanethiol and \ce{H2S} carry about equal shares (43--54\%
  and 43--46\% with trained-panel and population thresholds). Methanethiol is one fifth as concentrated, but its
  threshold is 5--6 times lower.
  \item With the composition of ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009}, or with the lowest reported thresholds, methanethiol
  carries 60--94\%.
\end{itemize}
The two datasets disagree on the concentrations themselves: \ce{H2S} is five times methanethiol in one and slightly
below it in the other, a difference ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009} attributes to the bean-rich diet of the first. In
that study's panel test, gas mixtures with methanethiol above its flatus threshold smelled like most passages.

Composition does not enter the silent/audible comparison: transport is linear and acts on all three gases alike, so
every ratio is the same for any composition. Absolute OAVs are 11--38\% lower with the second composition.

\emph{The intensity law with real flatus.} Zinc acetate lowers the summed OAV by a factor of 14. Stevens' law then
predicts rating ratios of 0.31--0.59, and the two judges gave 0.28 and 0.59 \cite{suarez1998}. Activated charcoal
lowers the OAV about 260-fold and is rated far weaker than the power law predicts, as power laws fail near threshold.
The silent/audible comparison uses supra-threshold ratios, where the law held.

\emph{What makes a passage rise or sink.} Composition sets most of the buoyancy (Figure~\ref{fig:chem}b).
\begin{itemize}
  \item For the median passage the compositional part of the reduced gravity ($+1.25$\,m\,s$^{-2}$ dry, $+1.40$
  saturated) is about three times the thermal part ($+0.47$).
  \item \ce{H2} and \ce{CH4} make flatus light and \ce{CO2} makes it heavy; the compositional part falls with \ce{CO2}
  content ($r = -0.80$).
  \item 6--12\% of passages sink even while hot. After cooling to room temperature, 25--38\% are heavier than air.
\end{itemize}
The dispersion model uses these signed values of $g'$.

\emph{Losses on the way to the nose} (Table~\ref{tab:reactivity}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item In an office, gas-phase oxidation removes at most 0.4\% of any of the three gases in 120\,s, and hypochlorite
  cleaning less than 4\%.
  \item Surface deposition is unconstrained by data. It removes 0.7\% in 120\,s at $v_d = 0.002$\,cm\,s$^{-1}$ and 51\%
  at 0.2\,cm\,s$^{-1}$. Treating the odorants as passive scalars therefore holds against chemistry by two to three
  orders of magnitude, and against deposition only if $v_d \lesssim 0.01$\,cm\,s$^{-1}$.
  \item Deposition would remove most from the silent release, which stays close to the body and the seat, and would
  lower its doses further in the simulated scenes.
\end{itemize}

\emph{How dilute a passage arrives.} The panel test of ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009} implies a dilution of
$0.6$--$13\times10^{4}$ between release and nose, depending on the threshold set. The breath-averaged dilution of the
analytic model at the neighbour has a median of $1.8\times10^{3}$ (10--90\%: $2.5\times10^{2}$--$2.9\times10^{4}$).
The panel value is biased upwards, because it requires the least sensitive panellist to detect but divides by the
thresholds of the most sensitive. The analytic model is at the concentrated end of the panel range, as intended for
an upper bound, and within about an order of magnitude of it.

\emph{Composition and volume.} Larger passages contain more fermentation gas and less nitrogen (per passage,
$r = +0.56$ for \ce{CO2} and $-0.50$ for \ce{N2}, $n = 87$), while the sulfur concentrations do not change with volume
($|r| \le 0.22$, not significant); sulfur mass therefore rises with passage volume \cite{suarez1997}. At high
production rates almost all flatus is fermentation gas \cite{tomlin1991}. The fermentation-gas fraction rises with
output under bean, fibre and lactulose challenges in every study that measured both ($r = +0.86$ to $+0.99$, computed
from the published tables \cite{steggerda1966,steggerda1968,calloway1969,odonnell1984,marthinsen1982,hickey1972}; in one
person from 26\% at 55\,mL to 79\% at 720\,mL per 4\,h \cite{levitt1976}), whereas on an ordinary diet larger outputs
carry more nitrogen \cite{kirk1949}. The density of the gas does not follow the amount: carbon dioxide and hydrogen
rise together, and the mean molar mass, 22--32\,g\,mol$^{-1}$ across diets, is set by the substrate's \ce{CO2}:\ce{H2}
ratio (its correlation with output ranges from $-0.37$ to $+0.90$ across seven studies). Composition therefore cannot
link audibility to the amount of gas, since it enters audibility only through density (Section~\ref{sec:si-acoustics}).

\section{Acoustics}
\label{sec:si-acoustics}
\emph{Duration and orifice.} Flatus passes during an anal relaxation lasting $19 \pm 6$\,s \cite{ronholt1999,corsetti2017}, which bounds the
duration $T$ from above, and the mid anal canal widens to about 8\,mm only at balloon pressures above those that drive
flatus \cite{luft2012,sorensen2014,miller1988b}, which bounds the effective diameter $d$.

\emph{Valve.}
The anal canal is modelled as a lip reed, a soft-tissue valve that self-oscillates in the flow through it, as the
lips do on a brass instrument. It oscillates when the driving pressure $\tfrac12\rho(u_0/C_\mathrm{d})^2$ exceeds a
threshold $P_\mathrm{th}$ = 0.6--2.5\,kPa (nominal 1.2\,kPa), with discharge coefficient $C_\mathrm{d} = 0.85$. The
closest measured soft-tissue sphincter source, the pharyngo-oesophageal segment of oesophageal speech, sustains
phonation down to 1.1--2.5\,kPa \cite{nagai1993,grolman2007,ng2011}, which bounds the onset from above; lips on a brass
instrument start at about 0.6\,kPa \cite{matteoli2021}. This gives an onset velocity
$u_\mathrm{onset} \approx 42$\,m\,s$^{-1}$ (21--71).

An oscillating valve radiates as a harmonic monopole with harmonics falling by 12\,dB per octave. Its fundamental is
taken as 160\,Hz (60--300\,Hz), between oesophageal speech at 60--150\,Hz \cite{nagai1993,robbins1984,pindzola1989,deschler1999}
and recordings of flatus near 270\,Hz, which are not peer-reviewed. A release counts as \emph{audible} when at least one harmonic exceeds both the ISO 226 hearing threshold
\cite{iso226_2023} and the masked threshold of the background, computed from the critical ratio \cite{hawkins1950}.
Backgrounds range from a 35\,dB(A) living room to a 77\,dB(A) subway car.

\emph{Valve state.} Manometry shows flatus passing through a relaxed sphincter at a rectal pressure rise of
0--15\,mmHg \cite{miller1988b,ronholt1999} but also against a contracted one \cite{kumar1990}. The 85\% silent share
treats the onset pressure as a property of the tissue and does not distinguish the two.

\begin{itemize}

  the scalar constant $K_c = 4.3$--4.6, with a grid uncertainty of at most 3.7\%. The $k$--$\omega$ SST model spreads

\section{Scene weights}
\label{sec:si-weights}
Weights are the hours per day an adult spends close to another person in each setting, with passages spread
uniformly over waking hours: sofa 1.4\,h (television with others in the room, from the American Time Use Survey;
bounds 0.8--2.0), elevator 0.01\,h (four shared rides of about 24\,s on working days; 0.0005--0.1) and standing
conversation 0.5\,h (0.2--1.5, an assumption from the talking and standing shares of the day), i.e.\ 0.73, 0.005 and
0.26. The home-centred bound takes the high sofa and low elevator and standing values, the office-centred bound the
reverse. Sources and derivation are in the repository (\texttt{refs/scene\_weights\_sources.md}). Within a scene,
postures and ventilation variants count equally.

\section{Breath sampling}
\label{sec:si-breath}
An audible puff reaches the neighbour after a median of 16\,s and passes with a time scale of about 3.9\,s
(10--90\%: 1.5--12\,s). Without a warning, the breathing phase barely matters: for a median passage the worst breath
captures 81--99\% of the peak. A listener warned by the sound needs about 25\,s of breath-hold for an even chance of
missing the puff (worst breath below 10\% of the peak); 33\,s, the typical maximum at end-expiration, gives 62\%.

\section{Origin of the odorants}
\label{sec:si-origin}
Two facts the transport model takes as given have chemical explanations in the literature; we did not test them.

\emph{Why sulfur concentration does not follow volume.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \ce{H2S} comes from sulfate-reducing bacteria and from cysteine degradation, whose genes are widespread in the
  gut microbiome \cite{gibson1993,braccia2021}. Sulfate reducers and methanogens both consume \ce{H2}, but whether
  they exclude each other is disputed. Sulfidogenic gut contents produced no methane in some studies
  \cite{gibson1993,gibson1993b}, whereas faecal sulfide did not differ between methane producers and non-producers in
  others \cite{strocchi1991,florin1991,levine1998}, and methanogenic faeces suppressed sulfide production sixfold in
  co-incubation \cite{strocchi1994}.
  \item The caecal mucosa of rats removes both gases quickly: methanethiol is demethylated to \ce{H2S}, and \ce{H2S} is
  oxidised mainly to thiosulfate \cite{levitt1999}. Rhodanese is the main detoxifying enzyme in human colon
  \cite{picton2002}, and SELENBP1 oxidises methanethiol with an apparent $K_m$ of 4.8\,nM \cite{pol2018}.
  \item Only free sulfide can enter the gas, and it is less than 5\% of faecal total sulfide \cite{tangerman2009}.
\end{itemize}
The odorants are thus trace products held at a balance of production and fast consumption, whereas passage volume is
set by \ce{N2}, \ce{CO2}, \ce{H2} and \ce{CH4}. We infer that this is why sulfur concentration and passage volume are
uncorrelated \cite{suarez1997}. Diet moves the balance: a meat-rich diet raised faecal sulfide from 0.22 to
3.38\,mmol\,kg$^{-1}$ in five volunteers \cite{magee2000}, although faecal sulfide reflects the binding capacity of the
stool as much as sulfide production \cite{levitt2002}, and the response to animal and plant diets differs between
people \cite{teigen2022}.

\emph{Diet can make large passages less sulfurous.} Within one diet, sulfur concentration does not change with passage
volume (Section~\ref{sec:si-chem}); across diets it can. Rapidly fermented fibres raised gas production up to
50-fold in faecal incubations while suppressing \ce{H2S} by up to 89\%, and cysteine raised \ce{H2S} 16-fold
\cite{yao2018}. Dietary sulfate tripled sulfide production and cut methane production fivefold, at unchanged stool
weight and transit, in three of six subjects \cite{christl1992b}; in sulfidogenic gut contents \ce{H2S} replaced
\ce{CH4} almost mole for mole \cite{gibson1993b}, diverting hydrogen from gas volume to sulfide \cite{grimble1989}.
Interventions aimed at sulfur move sulfur alone: bismuth cut faecal \ce{H2S} release 23-fold with total gas unchanged
\cite{suarez1998b}, and in dogs charcoal, yucca and zinc acetate cut \ce{H2S} by 38--86\% without changing gas
production or the number of emissions \cite{giffard2001}. Because audible passages are larger, a diet-driven link of
this kind would make audible passages less sulfurous per millilitre, in the direction of the belief. Its size in vivo
is unknown: no study has measured sulfur gases and flatus volume together across diets.

\emph{Why methanethiol smells foul.} The human receptor OR2T11 responds to short thiols, including methanethiol, in a
copper-dependent way, but not to \ce{H2S} \cite{li2016}. Human nasal mucus contains about 2.5\,\textmu M copper
\cite{block2017}, below the 30\,\textmu M added in the receptor assays, so the receptor's role in vivo is plausible
but not shown. Other receptors respond to sulfur ligands without copper \cite{duan2012,haag2019,zhang2018}. A
receptor that responds to methanethiol and not to \ce{H2S} would make methanethiol the characteristic note of flatus
independently of thresholds; we infer this, it has not been tested.


\section{Cross-cultural survey}
\label{sec:si-culture}
We searched dictionaries, proverb collections and etymological handbooks in 32 languages or historical stages for a
dedicated word for a silent passage and for an explicit claim that it smells worse, and checked every entry used
against a retrieved source. Each language is graded by its strongest evidence: A, an explicit saying (traditional,
or in media or film only); B, a dedicated word defined by its smell; C, a dedicated word without a smell claim; D,
nothing found. The silent/loud distinction is lexicalised in at least five language families. The explicit claim is
traditional only in Chinese and English, and smell-defined silent words arose independently at least four times
within Indo-European. The survey records whether the belief is documented in a language, not how many speakers hold
it.
