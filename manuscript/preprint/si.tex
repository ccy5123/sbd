\section{Odorant chemistry}
\label{sec:si-chem}
\subsection{Methods}
\emph{Odour activity.} Each sulfur gas contributes an odour activity value (OAV), its mole fraction over its detection
threshold, $\mathrm{OAV}_i = x_i/\theta_i$. The passage's OAV is their sum; we assume additivity and do not model
mixture suppression. We compare two measured compositions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item 87 passages of 16 volunteers \cite{suarez1998}, used throughout the dispersion model;
  \item 28 passages measured by gas chromatography: \ce{H2S} 149, methanethiol 216 and dimethyl sulfide
  144\,nmol\,L$^{-1}$ \cite{tangerman2009}.
\end{itemize}
Three threshold sets bracket the observer: a trained panel \cite{nagata2003}; population values, the geometric
means of detection thresholds for \ce{H2S} and methanethiol \cite{amoore1983} and, for dimethyl sulfide, the median
recognition threshold of an untrained panel of 35 \cite{wilby1969}; and the lowest values in Verschueren's handbook,
as tabulated in ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009}.

\emph{Removal check.} Zinc acetate precipitates \ce{H2S} and thiols; activated charcoal adsorbs all three gases. For both
treatments, ref.~\citenum{suarez1998} reports the gas concentrations and two judges' ratings before and after. We
predict the rating ratio as $(\mathrm{OAV}_\mathrm{after}/\mathrm{OAV}_\mathrm{before})^n$ with $n = 0.20$--$0.45$,
an assumed bracket around the 0.28 measured for canine flatus \cite{collins2001}, without fitting.

\emph{Buoyancy.} The reduced gravity $g' = g(\rho_a-\rho_g)/\rho_a$ of each of 16 measured compositions
\cite{suarez1997} is split into a thermal part, $g(1-T_a/T_g)$, for gas at 37\,\textdegree C in air at
22\,\textdegree C, and a compositional part, $g(M_a-M_g)/M_a$, at equal temperature with air at 50\% relative
humidity. The water-vapour content of flatus has not been measured, so we bracket dry and saturated gas.

\emph{Gas-phase and surface losses.} Gas-phase losses use IUPAC rate constants at 298\,K for reaction with OH,
\ce{O3}, \ce{NO3} and Cl \cite{atkinson2004}, with oxidant levels measured in an office: OH
$0.6$--$9.8\times10^{5}$\,cm$^{-3}$ and \ce{O3} 0.7--2.6\,ppb (daily means) \cite{fiorentino2023}. Two assumed upper cases add OH
$10^6$\,cm$^{-3}$ with \ce{O3} 40\,ppb, and Cl $10^6$\,cm$^{-3}$ during hypochlorite cleaning. No deposition velocity
$v_d$ has been published for these gases on indoor surfaces, so surface loss is bracketed as $k = v_d\,S/V$ with
$S/V = 3$\,m$^{-1}$.

\emph{Dilution in a panel test.} Ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009} reports the lowest source concentration that every panel
member detected when 100\,mL was released below the nose. Divided by a detection threshold, it gives the dilution
between release and nose in that test, which we compare with the breath-averaged dilution of the analytic model.

\subsection{Results}
\emph{Which molecule.} Over the two measured compositions and three threshold sets, methanethiol carries 43--94\% of
the summed OAV and never less than \ce{H2S} (Figure~\ref{fig:chem}a).
\begin{itemize}
  \item With the composition of ref.~\citenum{suarez1998}, methanethiol and \ce{H2S} carry about equal shares (43--54\%
  and 43--46\% with trained-panel and population thresholds). Methanethiol is one fifth as concentrated, but its
  threshold is 5--6 times lower.
  \item With the composition of ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009}, or with the lowest reported thresholds, methanethiol
  carries 60--94\%.
\end{itemize}
The two datasets disagree on the concentrations themselves: \ce{H2S} is five times methanethiol in one and slightly
below it in the other, a difference ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009} attributes to the bean-rich diet of the first. In
that study's panel test, gas mixtures with methanethiol above its flatus threshold smelled like most passages.

Composition does not enter the silent/audible comparison: transport is linear and acts on all three gases alike, so
every ratio is the same for any composition. Absolute OAVs are 11--38\% lower with the second composition.

\emph{The intensity law with real flatus.} Zinc acetate lowers the summed OAV by a factor of 14. Stevens' law then
predicts rating ratios of 0.31--0.59, and the two judges gave 0.28 and 0.59 \cite{suarez1998}. Activated charcoal
lowers the OAV about 260-fold and is rated far weaker than the power law predicts, as power laws fail near threshold.
The silent/audible comparison uses supra-threshold ratios, where the law held.

\emph{What makes a passage rise or sink.} Composition sets most of the buoyancy (Figure~\ref{fig:chem}b).
\begin{itemize}
  \item For the median passage the compositional part of the reduced gravity ($+1.25$\,m\,s$^{-2}$ dry, $+1.40$
  saturated) is about three times the thermal part ($+0.47$).
  \item \ce{H2} and \ce{CH4} make flatus light and \ce{CO2} makes it heavy; the compositional part falls with \ce{CO2}
  content ($r = -0.80$).
  \item 6--12\% of passages sink even while hot. After cooling to room temperature, 25--38\% are heavier than air.
\end{itemize}
The dispersion model uses these signed values of $g'$.

\emph{Losses on the way to the nose} (Table~\ref{tab:reactivity}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item In an office, gas-phase oxidation removes at most 0.4\% of any of the three gases in 120\,s, and hypochlorite
  cleaning less than 4\%.
  \item Surface deposition is unconstrained by data. It removes 0.7\% in 120\,s at $v_d = 0.002$\,cm\,s$^{-1}$ and 51\%
  at 0.2\,cm\,s$^{-1}$. Treating the odorants as passive scalars therefore holds against chemistry by two to three
  orders of magnitude, and against deposition only if $v_d \lesssim 0.01$\,cm\,s$^{-1}$.
  \item Deposition would remove most from the silent release, which stays close to the body and the seat, and would
  lower its doses further in the simulated scenes.
\end{itemize}

\emph{How dilute a passage arrives.} The panel test of ref.~\citenum{tangerman2009} implies a dilution of
$0.6$--$13\times10^{4}$ between release and nose, depending on the threshold set. The breath-averaged dilution of the
analytic model at the neighbour has a median of $1.6\times10^{3}$ (10--90\%: $2.6\times10^{2}$--$2.7\times10^{4}$).
The panel value is biased upwards, because it requires the least sensitive panellist to detect but divides by the
thresholds of the most sensitive. The analytic model is at the concentrated end of the panel range, as intended for
an upper bound, and within about an order of magnitude of it.

\emph{Composition and volume.} Larger passages contain more fermentation gas and less nitrogen (per passage,
$r = +0.56$ for \ce{CO2} and $-0.50$ for \ce{N2}, $n = 87$), while the sulfur concentrations do not change with volume
($|r| \le 0.22$, not significant); sulfur mass therefore rises with passage volume \cite{suarez1997}. At high
production rates almost all flatus is fermentation gas \cite{tomlin1991}. Under bean, fibre, fruit and lactulose challenges the
fermentation-gas fraction rises with output ($r = +0.86$ to $+0.98$ across diet or treatment means, computed from the
published tables \cite{steggerda1966,steggerda1968,calloway1969,odonnell1984,marthinsen1982,hickey1972}, for
ref.~\citenum{calloway1969} from the 12 control and bean rows without silicone; in one person from 26\% at 55\,mL to 79\% at 720\,mL per 4\,h \cite{levitt1976}). One study is an exception: on an ordinary
diet the fraction did not rise with output ($r = -0.41$ across 20 subjects), and after brussels sprouts the extra gas
was mostly nitrogen \cite{kirk1949}. The density of the gas does not follow the amount: carbon dioxide and hydrogen
rise together, and the mean molar mass, 22--32\,g\,mol$^{-1}$ across diets, is set by the substrate's \ce{CO2}:\ce{H2}
ratio (its correlation with output ranges from $-0.37$ to $+0.90$ across seven studies). Composition therefore cannot
link audibility to the amount of gas, since it enters audibility only through density (Section~\ref{sec:si-acoustics}).

\section{Acoustics}
\label{sec:si-acoustics}
\emph{Duration and orifice.} Flatus passes during an anal relaxation lasting $19 \pm 6$\,s \cite{ronholt1999} (relaxations accompanying
pan-colonic pressurizations last $23 \pm 4$\,s \cite{corsetti2017}), which bounds the duration $T$ from above, and the
mid anal canal widens to about 8\,mm only at balloon pressures (40--55\,mmHg) \cite{luft2012,sorensen2014} above the
rectal pressures at which flatus usually passes (median rise 8\,mmHg, range $-10$ to 88) \cite{miller1988b}, which
bounds the effective diameter $d$.

\emph{Valve.}
The anal canal is modelled as a lip reed, a soft-tissue valve that self-oscillates in the flow through it, as the
lips do on a brass instrument. It oscillates when the driving pressure $\tfrac12\rho(u_0/C_\mathrm{d})^2$ exceeds a
threshold $P_\mathrm{th}$ = 0.6--2.5\,kPa (nominal 1.2\,kPa), with discharge coefficient $C_\mathrm{d} = 0.85$. The
closest measured soft-tissue sphincter source, the pharyngo-oesophageal segment that drives oesophageal and
tracheo-oesophageal speech, phonates at 1.1--2.5\,kPa: sustained phonation from 1.1\,kPa \cite{nagai1993}, 1.8\,kPa
below the segment at minimum loudness \cite{grolman2007} and about 2.5\,kPa on average during speech (individuals
1.0--3.0\,kPa) \cite{ng2011}. This bounds the onset from above; lips on a brass
instrument start at about 0.6\,kPa \cite{matteoli2021}. This gives an onset velocity
$u_\mathrm{onset} \approx 42$\,m\,s$^{-1}$ (21--71).

An oscillating valve radiates as a harmonic monopole with harmonics falling by 12\,dB per octave. Its fundamental is
taken as 160\,Hz (60--300\,Hz), between oesophageal and tracheo-oesophageal speech at 60--150\,Hz \cite{nagai1993,robbins1984,pindzola1989,deschler1999}
and recordings of flatus near 270\,Hz, which are not peer-reviewed \cite{chervinsky2021}. A release counts as \emph{audible} when at least one harmonic exceeds both the ISO 226 hearing threshold
\cite{iso226_2023} and the masked threshold of the background, computed from the critical ratio \cite{hawkins1950}.
Backgrounds range from a 35\,dB(A) living room to a 77\,dB(A) subway car.

\emph{Valve state.} Manometry shows flatus passing through a relaxed sphincter, at a median rectal pressure rise
of 8\,mmHg (range $-10$ to 88; 0--15\,mmHg when flatus is only sensed) \cite{miller1988b}, with a fall in anal
pressure \cite{ronholt1999}, but also against a contracted sphincter \cite{kumar1990}. The 85\% silent share
treats the onset pressure as a property of the tissue and does not distinguish the two.

\section{Proofs of Propositions 1--3}
\label{sec:si-proofs}
% Main-text equation numbers, hard-coded: (1) deadliness, (2) Green's function, (3) Silencer number.
Equation numbers refer to the main text.

\emph{Proposition 1.} (i) follows from integrating Eq.~(2), $C = G*q$, over $t$. For (ii),
$C_\mathrm{s} = G*q_\mathrm{a}*\kappa = C_\mathrm{a}*\kappa$, and Young's inequality gives
$\|C_\mathrm{s}\|_\infty \le \|C_\mathrm{a}\|_\infty\,\|\kappa\|_1 = \|C_\mathrm{a}\|_\infty$. \hfill$\square$

A top-hat release lasting $nT$ is a top-hat lasting $T$ convolved with
$\kappa = n^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\delta(t-jT)$, so stretching a release by an integer factor is covered. The
average of the OAV over an inhalation starting at time $\tau$ is a convolution of $C$ with a non-negative kernel of
unit integral, so the same argument bounds its supremum over $\tau$. Eq.~(1) samples this function at the inhalations
of one breathing rhythm, so the bound applies to its largest value over the breathing phase and not to every fixed
phase: a short release can fall between two inhalations.

\emph{Proposition 2.} Let the release last $T_\mathrm{r}$, so that $q$ vanishes outside $[0, T_\mathrm{r}]$. Then
$C(t) = \int_0^{T_\mathrm{r}} G(t-s)\,q(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$, and as $t\to\infty$, $G(t-s)\to 1/V_\mathrm{room}$ uniformly
for $s\in[0,T_\mathrm{r}]$, so $C(t)\to V_\mathrm{room}^{-1}\int q\,\mathrm{d}s = V/V_\mathrm{room}$ for any $q$.
\hfill$\square$

\emph{Proposition 3.} On $\lnot H$, noticing requires smelling, so $\lnot H\cap N = \lnot H \cap S$ and
$P(S\mid\lnot H, N) = 1$. On $H$, $N$ is certain, so conditioning on $N$ adds nothing. \hfill$\square$

\section{Near field of the analytic model}
\label{sec:si-nearfield}
The release behaves as a starting jet. Its tip follows $x = \Gamma (\dot Q_0 u_0)^{1/4} t^{1/2}$, where
$\dot Q_0 = V/T$ is the volume flow rate and $\dot Q_0 u_0 = M_0$ the kinematic momentum flux, and once the source
stops it follows the puff law $x^4 = C_p^4 V u_0 t$, with $\Gamma = 2.8$ and $C_p = 2.6$ from the corrected analysis
\cite{diez2003} of the measurements of ref.~\citenum{sangras2002}. The centreline dilution is $x/(K_c d^*)$ with
$K_c = 5.11$ \cite{dowling1990}, where $d^* = d\,(\rho_0/\rho_\mathrm{a})^{1/2}$ is the momentum diameter of a release
of density $\rho_0$ into air of density $\rho_\mathrm{a}$. In parallel, a buoyant thermal grows with entrainment
coefficient $\alpha = 0.25$ \cite{landeau2014}, and the larger of the two dilutions is kept. The near field ends when
the self-induced velocity has fallen to the ambient velocity $u_h$ (0.05--0.25\,m\,s$^{-1}$).

For a steady jet, a starting jet and a puff alike, the momentum carried by the cloud is conserved while it entrains,
so the entrained mass grows as the inverse of the remaining velocity. At the hand-off velocity $u_h$ the dilution is
therefore proportional to $u_0/u_h$, which is Eq.~(3); the prefactor $\beta$ collects the jet or puff constants above
and is of order one.

\section{CFD validation}
\label{sec:si-validation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Free round jet} (axisymmetric, $\mathrm{Re} = 6700$, three meshes). The velocity-decay constant is 5.0 and
  the scalar constant $K_c = 4.3$--4.6, with a grid uncertainty of at most 3.7\%. The $k$--$\omega$ SST model spreads
  round jets about 25\% too fast, which over-dilutes audible releases by about 15\% and so favours the silent release.
  \item \emph{Convective boundary layer} of a simplified seated manikin with skin at 33\,\textdegree C, compared with
  thermal-manikin data \cite{licina2014}. The face-front peak velocity is 40--56\% too high at room temperatures of
  20--26\,\textdegree C; its change with room temperature is reproduced ($-27\%$ against $-33\%$ measured).
  \item \emph{Near-body source to breathing zone.} The chamber of ref.~\citenum{licina2017} (4.6 $\times$ 4.6 $\times$
  2.36\,m, 115\,m$^3$\,h$^{-1}$ through two ceiling supplies, exhaust at 1.8\,m) with the scene manikin, chair, skin
  temperature and solver; a 3.5\,L\,min$^{-1}$ source between the thighs at 0.5\,m runs for 10\,min. Intake fraction
  $\mathrm{iF} = Q_\mathrm{b}\int C_\mathrm{bz}\,\mathrm{d}t / m$ with $Q_\mathrm{b} = 0.5$\,m$^3$\,h$^{-1}$, from the
  breathing-zone probe at 1.1\,m; after 22\,min the room-mean level decays at the measured air-exchange rate. Result
  8.1, 9.1 and 11.0 per mille over 10, 22 and 100\,min against 5.5, 7.7 and 9.8 measured; relative to the well-mixed
  room 11.0 and 2.6 against 7.4 and 2.3. The manikin's convective output is 79\,W. Supply direction, supply
  temperature and exhaust size are not reported and were assumed (downward, room temperature, 0.3\,m square).
  \item \emph{Mesh.} The median silent release alone on the sofa mesh and on a mesh refined by 1.5 in every direction
  (0.72 and 2.2 million cells, same refinement levels): 150-s worst breath 0.42 and 1.28 at the releaser, 0.17 and
  0.094 at the neighbour. Released fourth in the four-release run on the base mesh, the same passage gives 0.33 and
  0.071, so the release order matters about as much as the mesh.
  \item \emph{Well-mixed tail.} Two coupled 10-minute runs of the sofa (one silent, one audible 92-mL release): the
  larger of the 150-s worst breath and the well-mixed level gives 1.00 and 1.20 (silent: releaser, neighbour) and
  0.84 and 1.12 (audible) of the actual worst breath at 600\,s, where the neighbours reach 0.83--0.89 of the level. At
  300\,s the rule overstates (1.56 and 1.28), so it is used for the 600-s window only. There is no such run for the
  standing rooms, whose bystander result rests on the well-mixed level alone.
\end{itemize}

\section{CFD scenes}
\label{sec:si-scenes}
All scenes use the solver of the main text, skin at 33\,\textdegree C and walls at 23\,\textdegree C. Each runs 90\,s
without a release; the four representative releases then follow 30\,s apart (strongest first) on separate scalars,
each until 150\,s after its start. A release enters behind the releaser's buttocks, pointing down and back, through a
1\,cm-radius inlet (area $A_e$) at its own momentum flux $M_0$, and so at the velocity $u_e = (M_0/A_e)^{1/2}$
instead of $u_0$. Room air pre-dilutes it by $u_0/u_e = 2r_e/d$ (Table~\ref{tab:releases}), and the odorant
concentration and the equivalent temperature excess of the flatus buoyancy ($g' = 1.70$\,m\,s$^{-2}$) are reduced by
the same factor, so the momentum, odorant and buoyancy fluxes are kept and the volume flux is not. A jet conserves its
momentum while it entrains, so its dilution when it has slowed to the ambient velocity $u_h$ is about $u_0/u_h$
either way (Section~\ref{sec:si-nearfield}).

\section{Scene weights}
\label{sec:si-weights}
Weights are the hours per day an adult spends close to another person in each setting, with passages spread
uniformly over waking hours: sofa 1.4\,h (television with others in the room, from the American Time Use Survey;
bounds 0.8--2.0), lift 0.01\,h (four shared rides of about 24\,s on working days; 0.0005--0.1) and standing
conversation 0.5\,h (0.2--1.5, an assumption from the talking and standing shares of the day), i.e.\ 0.73, 0.005 and
0.26. The home-centred bound takes the high sofa and low lift and standing values, the office-centred bound the
reverse. Sources and derivation are in the repository (\texttt{refs/scene\_weights\_sources.md}). Within a scene,
postures and ventilation variants count equally.

\section{Robustness of the headline}
\label{sec:si-robust}

share in which the silent one is stronger (above the just-noticeable ratio) and the share in which the audible one is.

\section{Breath sampling}
\label{sec:si-breath}
An audible puff reaches the neighbour after a median of 16\,s and passes with a time scale of about 3.7\,s
(10--90\%: 1.4--11.5\,s). Without a warning, the breathing phase barely matters: for a median passage the worst breath
captures 80--99\% of the peak. A listener warned by the sound needs about 25\,s of breath-hold for an even chance of
missing the puff (worst breath below 10\% of the peak); 33\,s, the typical maximum at end-expiration, gives 63\%.

\section{Origin of the odorants}
\label{sec:si-origin}
Two facts the transport model takes as given have chemical explanations in the literature; we did not test them.

\emph{Why sulfur concentration does not follow volume.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \ce{H2S} comes from sulfate-reducing bacteria \cite{gibson1993} and from cysteine degradation, whose genes are
  widespread in the gut microbiome \cite{braccia2021}. Sulfate reducers and methanogens both consume \ce{H2}, but
  whether they exclude each other is disputed. Sulfidogenic gut contents produced no methane in some studies
  \cite{gibson1993,gibson1993b}, whereas faecal sulfide did not differ between methane producers and non-producers in
  others \cite{strocchi1991,florin1991}, \ce{H2S} release from incubated faeces did not correlate with methane release
  ($r = 0.11$) \cite{levine1998}, and methanogenic faeces suppressed sulfide production sixfold in co-incubation
  \cite{strocchi1994}.
  \item The caecal mucosa of rats removes both gases quickly: methanethiol is demethylated to \ce{H2S}, and \ce{H2S} is
  oxidised mainly to thiosulfate \cite{levitt1999}. Rhodanese is the main detoxifying enzyme in human colon
  \cite{picton2002}, and SELENBP1 oxidises methanethiol with an apparent $K_m$ of 4.8\,nM \cite{pol2018}.
  \item Only free sulfide can enter the gas, and it is less than 5\% of faecal total sulfide (free methanethiol is
  22--54\% of the total) \cite{tangerman2009}.
\end{itemize}
The odorants are thus trace products held at a balance of production and fast consumption, whereas passage volume is
set by \ce{N2}, \ce{CO2}, \ce{H2} and \ce{CH4}. We infer that this is why sulfur concentration and passage volume are
uncorrelated \cite{suarez1997}. Diet moves the balance: a meat-rich diet raised faecal sulfide from 0.22 to
3.38\,mmol\,kg$^{-1}$ in five volunteers \cite{magee2000}, although faecal sulfide reflects the binding capacity of the
stool as much as sulfide production \cite{levitt2002}, and the response to animal and plant diets differs between
people \cite{teigen2022}.

\emph{Diet can make large passages less sulfurous.} Within one diet, sulfur concentration does not change with passage
volume (Section~\ref{sec:si-chem}); across diets it can. In faecal incubations, rapidly fermented fibres produced up to
50 times more gas than poorly fermented fibre while suppressing \ce{H2S} by up to 89\%, and cysteine raised \ce{H2S}
16-fold \cite{yao2018}. Dietary sulfate tripled sulfide production and cut methane production fivefold, at unchanged stool
weight and transit, in three of six subjects \cite{christl1992b}; in the sulfidogenic gut contents of one
autopsy subject, \ce{H2S} took the place of the \ce{CH4} found in a methanogenic one \cite{gibson1993b}, diverting hydrogen from gas volume to sulfide \cite{grimble1989}.
Interventions aimed at sulfur move sulfur alone: bismuth cut faecal \ce{H2S} release 23-fold with total gas unchanged
\cite{suarez1998b}, and charcoal, yucca and zinc acetate cut \ce{H2S} production by 38--86\% without changing gas
production in dog faecal incubations, and fed to dogs did not change the number of emissions \cite{giffard2001}. Because audible passages are larger, a diet-driven link of
this kind would make audible passages less sulfurous per millilitre, in the direction of the belief. Its size in vivo
is unknown: no study has measured sulfur gases and flatus volume together across diets.

\emph{Why methanethiol smells foul.} The human receptor OR2T11 responds to short thiols, including methanethiol, in a
copper-dependent way, but did not respond to \ce{H2S} in that assay \cite{li2016}. Human nasal mucus contains about 2.5\,\textmu M copper
\cite{block2017}, below the 30\,\textmu M added in the receptor assays, so the receptor's role in vivo is plausible
but not shown. Other receptors respond to sulfur ligands without copper \cite{duan2012,haag2019,zhang2018}. A
receptor that responds to methanethiol and not to \ce{H2S} would make methanethiol the characteristic note of flatus
independently of thresholds; we infer this, it has not been tested.


\section{Cross-cultural survey}
\label{sec:si-culture}
We searched dictionaries, proverb collections and etymological handbooks in 32 languages or historical stages for a
dedicated word for a silent passage and for an explicit claim that it smells worse, and checked every entry used
against a retrieved source. Each language is graded by its strongest evidence: A, an explicit saying (traditional,
or in media or film only); B, a dedicated word defined by its smell; C, a dedicated word without a smell claim; D,
nothing found. The silent/loud distinction is lexicalised in at least five language families. The explicit claim is
traditional only in Chinese and English, and smell-defined silent words arose independently at least four times
within Indo-European. The survey records whether the belief is documented in a language, not how many speakers hold
it.
