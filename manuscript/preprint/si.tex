\section{CFD validation}
\label{sec:si-validation}
\src{cfd/validation/m3\_report.md}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Free round jet} (axisymmetric, $\mathrm{Re} = 6700$, three meshes). This gives a velocity-decay
  constant of 5.0 and a scalar constant $K_c = 4.3$--4.6, with grid uncertainty of at most 3.7\%. The known
  round-jet anomaly of SST (about 25\% excess spreading) means the model over-dilutes audible jets by about 15\%,
  which favours the silent release.
  \item \emph{Convective boundary layer} of a simplified seated manikin with skin at 33\,\textdegree C, compared with
  thermal-manikin data \cite{licina2014}. The face-front peak velocity is 40--56\% too high at room temperatures of
  20--26\,\textdegree C, while the trend with room temperature is reproduced ($-27\%$ vs $-33\%$ measured). The fast
  boundary layer shortens travel times to the releaser's own nose by about 30\%; the analytic model compensates by
  stretching the CFD times by a factor of 1.4.
\end{itemize}

\section{CFD scenes}
\label{sec:si-scenes}
All scenes use the solver of the main text (transient RANS $k$--$\omega$ SST, Boussinesq, internal-energy form), skin
at 33\,\textdegree C and walls at 23\,\textdegree C. Manikins are built from geometric primitives. The release is a
momentum-equivalent inlet behind the releaser's buttocks, pointing down and back: silent, 92\,mL over 2\,s through
8\,mm ($u_0 = 0.9$\,m\,s$^{-1}$); audible, 92\,mL over 0.5\,s through 2.4\,mm (41\,m\,s$^{-1}$). The odorant is a
passive scalar, sampled at probes 2.5\,cm in front of each nose, on 5\,cm breathing-zone discs at nose height and
over sinusoidal breathing cycles. Each scene first runs a precursor that develops the body plumes; the silent and
audible branches then start from the same field.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Sofa duet.} A 3.2 $\times$ 3.2 $\times$ 2.6\,m living room at 23\,\textdegree C with a 0.5\,m ceiling
  vent for pressure relief [ASSUMPTION]. Two seated manikins sit 0.6\,m apart on a two-seat sofa whose base is solid
  to the floor. Mesh 0.72 million cells, refined to 5\,mm at the release and 2.5\,mm on the inlet face. Precursor
  30\,s; branches 30\,s, the upright one extended to 120\,s. Two posture variants rotate the releaser's upper body
  about the hip: reclined by 4.5\,\textdegree, so that the back closes onto the backrest, and leaning forward by
  10\,\textdegree, so that the gap behind the back opens \src{cfd/cases/sofa/m4\_report.md}.
  \item \emph{Elevator.} The car of a 630\,kg, eight-person elevator, 1.10\,m wide, 1.40\,m deep and 2.10\,m high (a
  manufacturer's standard car, within the maximum area of 1.66\,m$^2$ allowed for that load). Four standing manikins
  (nose at 1.59\,m) stand in a $2 \times 2$ grid facing the door; the fan is off and a 0.2\,m ceiling opening
  relieves pressure [ASSUMPTION]. Mesh 0.91 million cells. Precursor 30\,s; four 60\,s branches release behind a
  front-row or a back-row occupant, and mirror symmetry gives the other two positions
  \src{cfd/cases/elevator/m5\_report.md}.
  \item \emph{Standing pair.} The living room of the sofa duet with two standing manikins face to face, noses 1.0\,m
  apart, and 0.8\,m of open floor behind each back. Two air states share one mesh (0.43 million cells): still air, and
  office ventilation through one square ceiling diffuser above the pair, 21.2\,L\,s$^{-1}$ = 2.9 air changes per hour
  (EN 16798-1 Category II for two people in a low-polluting 10\,m$^2$ room: 7\,L\,s$^{-1}$ per person plus
  0.7\,L\,s$^{-1}$\,m$^{-2}$ \cite{olesen2024}), discharged horizontally along the ceiling at 1.5\,m\,s$^{-1}$
  [ASSUMPTION] at room temperature, with a ceiling exhaust grille. Precursors 30\,s (still) and 90\,s (ventilated);
  60\,s branches \src{cfd/cases/standing}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Sofa duet}
\label{sec:si-sofa}
\emph{Silent release.} The silent release has too little momentum to leave the convective boundary layer of the
releaser's back. It is drawn up the gap between back and backrest, over the head and on to the ceiling. Within 30\,s
its breathing-zone OAV stays at or below $10^{-10}$ at both noses, while the region within 25\,cm of the releaser's
head reaches an OAV of 6\,000.

\emph{Audible release.} The audible jet splashes into the corner between seat and backrest, spreads sideways along
the gap and is lifted up both backs as a wider, more dilute sheet; part of it curls around the heads. It reaches the
neighbour's head region within 6\,s and both breathing zones at OAV 0.2--0.6.

\emph{Reclined posture.} Reclining the releaser by 4.5\,\textdegree\ closes the back onto the backrest. The silent
release then leaves the seat-level wedge sideways and still rises behind the head, so no breathing zone receives a
detectable dose. The audible jet now wraps around the releaser's face, exceeding threshold at their own nose (OAV
2--4).

\emph{Forward lean.} Leaning the releaser forward by 10\,\textdegree\ opens the gap behind the back. Both releases
then rise behind the head, and the releaser's nose, now 13\,cm further forward, receives neither (breathing-zone OAV
$\le 10^{-5}$). The silent cloud reaches the region within 25\,cm of the neighbour's head as strongly as the audible
one (OAV 580 against 450) but not the neighbour's breathing zone ($10^{-4}$); the audible jet reaches it at OAV 2.6.

\emph{Longer window.} Extending the upright case to 120\,s shows what happens after the direct pass. Both clouds reach
the ceiling, come back down through the room and arrive at both noses from about 45\,s on. After about 70\,s the
silent and audible traces at the neighbour are indistinguishable (worst breath 0.39 for both), as Theorem~2
requires. At the releaser's own nose the mixed silent cloud runs 1.5--2 times higher and touches the trained-panel
threshold for about a second near 100\,s. These late levels are 10--23 times below the thresholds of an untrained
observer, and they arrive when the release can no longer be attributed to anyone.

\section{Elevator}
\label{sec:si-elevator}
\emph{What stands behind the culprit decides.} A silent release follows the culprit's own back plume.
\begin{itemize}
  \item With a person behind (front-row culprit), it rises in the 0.2\,m gap between the culprit's back and that
  person's face, straight into their breathing zone. The silent release is stronger at every nose
  ($R = 1.4$--$4.3$), and strongest, OAV 27, for the person behind.
  \item With the car wall behind (back-row culprit), it rises in the gap between back and wall, the chimney of the
  sofa, and reaches the front row only below threshold. The audible jet, which hits the floor and is lifted by all
  four body plumes, is now stronger at every nose ($R = 0.20$--$0.55$).
\end{itemize}

\emph{Who smelt it.} With a front-row culprit, the person behind smells it first (8.8\,s), before the culprit
(17.6\,s). The folk rule that the first to smell it dealt it is therefore right only for a back-row culprit, i.e.\
in half of the positions, for both modes.

\emph{Can the others tell?} Each occupant other than the culprit reacts with the detection probability of the main
text's observer models, independently; observers see only who reacted and infer the culprit with a uniform prior,
for a culprit who stays still and one who reacts along. An everyday, distracted car notices about nine passages in
ten not at all. When someone does react, the culprit is identified barely above chance (0.28--0.31, against 0.25).
Attentive occupants do better, but only about one time in two (0.39--0.52). Odour gives no better identification
for silent passages than for audible ones; the audible passage also carries the culprit's acoustic signature, which
is not modelled.

\section{Breath sampling}
\label{sec:si-breath}
An audible puff reaches the neighbour after a median of 16\,s and passes with a time scale of about 3.8\,s
(10--90\%: 1.4--12\,s). Unwarned, the breathing phase barely helps: for a median passage the worst breath still
captures 81--99\% of the peak. A listener warned by the sound needs about 23\,s of breath-hold for an even chance of
missing the puff (worst breath below 10\% of the peak); 33\,s, the typical maximum at end-expiration, gives 68\%
\src{perception/m6\_report.md}.

\section{Cross-cultural survey}
\label{sec:si-culture}
We searched dictionaries, proverb collections and etymological handbooks in 32 languages or historical stages for a
dedicated word for a silent passage and for an explicit claim that it smells worse; every entry used was checked
against a fetched source \src{culture/survey\_summary.md, culture/cross\_cultural\_table.csv}. Each language is graded
by its strongest evidence: A, an explicit saying (traditional, or in media or film only); B, a dedicated word defined by
its smell; C, a dedicated word without a smell claim; D, nothing found. The silent/loud distinction is lexicalised in
at least five language families; the explicit claim is traditional only in Chinese and English, and smell-defined
silent words arose independently at least four times within Indo-European. Documentary presence is not prevalence.
