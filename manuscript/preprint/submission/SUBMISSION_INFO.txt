ABSTRACT (plain text)
The belief that a silent passage of flatus smells worse than an audible one appears in many languages but is untested.
We follow a passage from sound through dispersion to a bystander's memory, with a lip-reed model of the anal valve and
literature dispersion and observer models. Most releases, 85%, never set the valve oscillating. We prove that a slower
release of the same gas cannot give a higher peak while both are carried passively by the same flow. In flow
simulations of seated and standing people, the warm air rising along the body takes a slow release and lets a fast one
pass, so momentum decides which nose a release reaches. With passages as emitted, a bystander on a sofa finds the
silent passage worse in 0.5% of pairs and the audible one in 9.2%, an order robust to numerical uncertainty; only in a
closed lift is the silent one worse as often. A silent passage is noticed only if smelled, and this selection alone
can explain the belief: among remembered pairs the silent passage seems worse in 88%. It predicts that releasers
believe it less firmly than bystanders and that anosmic people never remember a silent passage.
