it]" for publication as a Research Article in *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*.

The belief that a silent release of flatus smells worse than an audible one is recorded in many languages, yet no
mechanistic test of it exists. The closest prior work is a conference abstract on the physics of flatulence
(Ancalle et al. 2021). We follow one passage through a single chain: chemistry, sound, dispersion, perception and
memory. Three results reach beyond the topic.

1. **Routing by the human convective boundary layer.** The warm air rising along a body takes a slow release next
   to it and lets a fast one pass, so the momentum of the release decides which nearby nose it reaches. This bears
   on any near-body source, such as respiratory, skin and clothing emissions in shared indoor spaces.
2. **A peak bound for linear transport.** We prove that a slower release of the same substance cannot give a higher
   peak concentration, in any room or flow. Only the non-linear near field lets it win, by a factor bounded by a
   velocity ratio, which we call the Silencer number.
3. **Selection across two senses.** When an event can be noticed through either of two senses, selection on noticing
   alone can create a false sensory belief. Applied here, it can explain the belief even though, as emitted, a
   silent passage is the stronger one in only [GAP: v4] of pairs. It makes testable predictions: releasers should
   hold the belief less firmly than bystanders, and people without a sense of smell should never remember a silent
   passage.

The work joins fluid dynamics, chemistry, acoustics, sensory science and cognition. We think it suits *Interface*
because each part can be read on its own, and because the question shows how a physical and a cognitive explanation
can be separated.

Equations and proofs are in appendices, and technical details are in the electronic supplementary material. Code and
